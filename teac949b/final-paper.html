I Am Poem1

I am creative and empathetic

I wonder how I can use my newly acquired knowledge to facilitate meaningful change

I hear the stories of pain and hope within my community

I see the boot heel of oppression on the neck of my students and peers

I want to open the door for others and support non-Western funds of knowledge

I ask

I am creative and empathetic

I pretend that my contributions are more meaningful than they are or should be

I feel despair about our current state of education but simultaneously hopeful for our youth

I touch the ground and the sky

I worry that our efforts will not be enough

I cry for the futures cut short

I ask

I am creative and empathetic

I understand that progress is not linear

I say all knowledge and ways of knowing have value

I dream about the day that our students of color do not have to live in fear

I try to provide a safe space for my students and challenge my own internal biases

I hope for the day where education is more human

I ask

I am creative and empathetic

My journey to becoming an educator is an unusual one and certainly non-traditional. I was homeschooled from first grade through my senior year and my kindergarten was a Montessori school attached to the family Catholic church. My mother did what would probably be called “unschooling” today, where she knew what concepts and topics she wanted us to learn but wrapped them into themes and projects that appealed to our interests at the time. Even with the bumps in the road from incorporating the wisdom booklets from the Advanced Training Institute and the foray into creationist science and history my first couple of years of high school, I have primarily fond memories of my education and feel that I was more prepared for college and even life beyond school to some degree than many of my peers based on my education. What I didn’t realize until taking this class was that she was doing a very mild form of youth participatory action research (YPAR) with us and the rest of the homeschool group we were in. Cammarota et al. (2017) define and summarize YPAR in the paragraph below cited from page three of their Youth Community Leaders curriculum:

“Youth participatory action research (YPAR) is a pedagogical process that cultivates the recognition of oppression through reflection and action or what Brazilian scholar and philosopher Paulo Freire calls ‘praxis.’ It is through praxis that young people of color transform their perspective into critical consciousness. Linking praxis to YPAR suggests that research and pedagogical objectives center on challenging and overcoming an unjust social order. Through reflection and action, youth illuminate how societal institutions, such as schools, can shift from sites of oppression to spaces of liberation.  They look to challenge dominant representations of their identity and culture attempting to perpetuate their subordination. The challenge leads to new visions of education that foster their voices, community commitments, and social activism.”

While my experiences as a student using what I will call YPAR-light had very little focus on race explicitly, my education had a strong focus on social justice. We knew that we were privileged, that our family was more than comfortable on my father’s single income, and we were raised to use our privilege to help others. This was not with a focus on disrupting the dominant culture as that was not how my parents perceived the world, but as a focus on using our skills and resources to create “micro-disruptions” in the community by focusing on individuals and small communities. This approach, to me, never felt adequate, and when I finally reached the stage in my education as a teacher where I was beginning to question the status quo (thank you Dr. Pearson!) I was introduced to culturally relevant teaching and pedagogy, and I realized that I needed to be much more intentional about disrupting and dismantling the traditional systems of power and racism present in education, especially mathematics. From both my personal experience as a student, and now those as an educator, the statement made by Stavrou and Miller (2017) that the “Western worldview regards mathematical knowledge as being uncontestable, objective, and dissociated from experience, history, and cultures” (p. 95) rings uncomfortably true for it being the year 2023. My goal with this self-study and reflection is to propose a new way of looking at and teaching mathematics and incorporating technology that is not only more inclusive to all students but also helps students challenge the dominant order to foment change beyond the classroom.

YPAR and Counternarratives as Math Curriculum

Gonzales et al. (2021) state that their research “allowed the non-youth authors to center the narratives and experiences of students as an invaluable expertise” (p. 74) and go on to say that because of this they write as a “collective us.” YPAR throws out the traditional banking model of education and instead challenges educators to take a “step back and support the creative genius naturally ingrained in youth” (Gonzalez et al, 2017, p. 82). This is especially important in the field of mathematics because it is often considered completely immutable, objective, and True and this mindset can be incredibly dehumanizing to students as they are being required to learn something that is explicitly denying their knowledge and life experiences. Martinez and Yeong I (2019) assert that for a student to be successful in mathematics they must “see themselves as capable and powerful mathematical learners when participating in mathematics in personal and social ways” (p. 252) and go on to suggest that YPAR is a highly effective method of creating a learning environment where this outcome is possible.

Yeh et al. (2021) go on to say that for YPAR to be effective we as mathematics educators need to “navigate complex terrain and search beyond privileged spaces to locate knowledge from communities that disrupt colonial systems of knowledge making” (p. 73). Both Martinez and I (2019) and Yeh et al. (2021) discuss how mathematics treats identity formation as an individual activity, but it is also shaped and supported (or not) by our families, cultures, and their collective experiences. Specifically, Yeh et al. (2021) reference Anzaldua’s concept of borderlands consciousness and say that acknowledging, supporting, and centering this type of knowing can “empower educators and students of color in that they recognize the advantage of having insights into the experiences of the Oppressed and the Oppressors and may reveal the cracks that support social change” (p. 81).

Torre and Ayala (2009) call this type of learning and knowing “theoriz[ing] from the flesh” (p. 387) and also reference Anzaldua’s concept of Borderlands consciousness in their work towards creating liberatory YPAR within the classroom. Particularly impactful is their statement that “challenging traditional notions of expertise, PAR assumes that everyday people carry deep knowledge and analysis about the conditions of their lives and should lead in determining meaningful research questions, designs, methods, analyses and products” (p. 388). YPAR is an inherently disruptive epistemology and pedagogy in that it removes the researcher and/or teacher as “expert” and instead recenters the voices, stories, and knowledge of those who are typically considered passive objects to be studied or taught. For YPAR to be successful the participants must be from the stakeholders within the community, school, or classroom and a community of safety, mutual respect, and collaboration must already be in place. Without these things, true YPAR is not possible.

Mathematics and Technology

Technology has become ever-present in our world, and especially after the COVID-19 pandemic, technology has become a staple in K-12 classrooms. The implementation of and access to this technology has been somewhat patchwork across the country and varies between districts, schools, and often even by classroom. This patchwork implementation and access is a serious equity issue is a “central concern when considering the effective teaching and learning of mathematics” (Ruef et al., 2022, p. 1). Ruef et al. (2022) also pointed out that COVID-19 exposed and exacerbated inequities seen with specifically online learning and that “students were challenged in finding new ways of connecting in online class settings, and that opportunities to learn were mediated by access to, and fluency with, technology” (p. 2). The primary focus of their research was how teachers and students establish and sustained caring and supportive relationships with online education, and as mentioned above, such relationships are crucial for true YPAR to take place within the classroom. Interestingly, one of the largest challenges the author’s discovered was the dearth of effective digital, technological tools stating, “despite advances in graphing software, mathematics education retains its focus on pencil-and-paper activities” (Ruef et al., 2021, p. 5). Eppard et al. (2022) looked specifically at iPad integration into the math classroom and stated that “the iPad transforms students’ learning experience by providing instant access to important educational resources anywhere and anytime” (p. 374). They go on to suggest that the use of iPads in the math classroom supports student-centered learning because “the tablet devices provide students with a more interactive and collaborative learning experience” (p. 375). However, the students in this study were all Indian nationals living in Dubai and attending a private school for Indian nationals. This is not a particularly representative population and the authors themselves acknowledge that the use of iPads removes most collaboration within the classroom and that that is not an ideal learning environment for many students.

Rubin (1999) wrote that “rather than looking at math education from the perspective of the computer, we must look at computers from the perspective of mathematics education” and continues saying, “the role of technology in the math classroom must be in service of goals we hold for students’ mathematical knowledge and expertise” (p. 1). Feng et al. (2023) in their report on the impact of technology-supported learning on long-term math learning echoes Rubin’s statements from nearly 25 years ago with the finding that technology-based programs have significant potential to improve math learning for all students. They offer the ever-important caveat to this finding by cautioning that technology-based programs must be supplemental and “aligned to class instruction” (p. 17). Unlike Eppard et al., Feng et al. conducted their research in diverse and primarily urban schools, but they solicited no input or feedback from the students or parents, which Eppard et al. did collect to some degree in their research. What is clear from this small selection of research is that mathematics research is still quite centered around traditional Western epistemologies, and the research participants are passive objects to be studied, measured, and acted upon. Stavrou and Miller (2017) argue for more de-colonial research methods within the field of mathematics with the statement that “applying decolonizing strategies to mathematics will make visible that is a subject developed in time and place and will expose the imperialistic operation that deprives mathematics of its historical roots and human construction” (p. 106). Technology has the potential to be effective tool in working to decolonize mathematics education, but that will never be the case with the current research and deficit mindsets surrounding the use of technology in the classroom.

Mathematics and Student Motivation

Abu and Kribushi (2022) attempt to bridge the gap between the perceived lack of student motivation surrounding math utilizing technology after observing that, “the use of technology has not led to a revolution in teaching or learning” (p. 3). In their study they used a digital writing board as a method of potentially increasing student motivation in the math classroom. While the study found some increase in student self-reported motivation, they did note that student achievement scores did significantly increase as a result of using the digital writing board. Their conclusion was that the “use of [a] digital board affects the learning climate” (Abu and Kribushi, 2022, p. 9). This again points to the effectiveness of the principles of YPAR, because it was not so much the technology that increased student motivation and academic achievement, but its appropriate implementation creating a more positive and collaborative learning environment. Alrajeh and Shindel (2020) further support this theme by stating that “instructional support was the most dominant factor [in] determining student engagement” (p. 177).

Light and Pierson (2014) specifically looked at Khan Academy as a technological instructional support that might increase student engagement. Khan Academy is a gamification model of mathematical learning and gamification is intended to be the external motivating factor to increase student motivation and engagement with mathematical learning. According to Light and Pierson (2014), “educational policy makers around the globe often talk about Khan Academy with great expectations for deeply transforming teaching and learning, with the concept of ‘flipping the classroom’ where students receive direct instruction via video outside of the classroom and then work with the teacher doing math in the classroom” (p. 117). They go onto point how this use of technology is a large equity issue as it assumes a level of access to technology and connectivity that many families do not have access to or can afford. Their findings were that “teachers embedding Khan Academy resources within their traditional instructional practices and creating a decidedly non-traditional—yet improved—learning environment” had the most practical and positive outcomes (p. 117).

Finally, Niemi et al. (2018) incorporated digital storytelling (DST) into the math classroom in an attempt to increase student engagement and math literacy. Niemi et al. (2018) chose a collaborative model of DST as they felt that “learning through DST is a socially and culturally related process that occurs when there are interactions among learners, material tools, psychological tools, and other people” (p. 333). Their findings indicated that DST is an effective “pedagogical means for using digital technology to motivate students to become active learners” (p. 347). This study took place within two distinctly different educational contexts and cultures (Chinese and Finnish) and the results were similar in both contexts. This study is also one of the few that comes close to approximating anything even related to YPAR. The implication here is that a collaborative learning environment focused on students’ funds of knowledge and using that knowledge for change is one of the most effective ways to cultivate student motivation and engagement in the classroom.

Conclusion

Overall, the state of mathematics educational research is still very quantitative in nature, and “data” focused. However, as Martin et al. (2017) have noted there is beginnings of a “sociopolitical turn [emphasis original] in mathematics education” and there is an “increased attention to the political aspects of mathematics education, including knowledge, power, and identity” (p. 607). Youth Participatory Action Research has started to gain a foothold in mathematics education and shows promise as both an epistemology and ontology that can transform mathematics education. Stavrou and Miller (2017) sum it up best by saying, “we believe that if researchers and educators acknowledge the root causes of inequality, then there would be a shift in the literature away from positing a lack of cultural relevance as the reason that Indigenous students are disengaged with mathematics” (p. 110). While their research, focused on Aboriginal and Indigenous students, this statement applies to all students learning mathematics.
