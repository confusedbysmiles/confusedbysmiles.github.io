TEAC 882D

Artificial Intelligence and APIs in the Design of Learning Experiences

University of Nebraska–Lincoln | 3 credit hours | Artifacts online

What this course was

The semester after ChatGPT went public. Week 2 was titled “Chatbots, Society, and Education,” and the course ran on the assumption that the thing everyone was suddenly talking about would need to be designed with rather than around. This is the earliest formal AI coursework in my program, and the first time a generative model appears in anything I submitted — slide 3 of the final project opens “According to Chat GPT…” and quotes it as a source on how learning analytics are used in US schools. It was not a very interesting source. That was rather the point.

The final project argued that learning analytics fail individual students by design: they find patterns in aggregates and apply them to a person, which works only for people who already resemble the aggregate. Against that I set narrative assessment — a written account of what a learner actually did and how it went — and asked what AI might be good for if not for ranking. Compiling a body of one student’s work. Looking for patterns within that student rather than between students. Helping write the narrative. The deck ends with a national core curriculum, learner profiles instead of grades, and a slide titled “A Pipedream.”

Dissertation corpus

Through line

Slide 10 asks “Which one tells you more?” and answers with two documents side by side: a Grade 11 course table — ten courses, credits, letter grades, a GPA — and the year-end narrative report written for the same year. Both are mine. They are from my own homeschool record, 2002–2003. I put my own education on the slide as the evidence, in April 2023, without noticing that I had done anything except find a convenient example.

That is reading one’s own formation as data — the dissertation’s central move — and it is the earliest instance in the archive. Earlier than the TEAC 949B paper that autumn, where I had previously dated it. Earlier than any autoethnography reading. The identifying header on the report is redacted in the published deck; the narrative body, which is what the slide was ever about, is intact.

Slide 9 describes what AI might do instead of ranking: compile a body of one learner’s work, look for patterns intra-student rather than inter-student, help write the narrative. Slide 14’s action item is “AI tool to create learning profiles based on multi-modal inputs.” Three years later, that is a fair description of the dissertation tracker — except that the learner it profiles is me. The pattern repeats across the program: every tool I designed for students, I eventually turned on myself. The intra-student argument on slide 9 is also, word for word, the methodological case for a study with one participant.
