Item banks — three subscales

Subscale 1 — Previous Experiences as Learners

Math Teacher Identity Instrument

Subscale 1 — Previous Experiences as a Learner: Item Bank

Construct: A teacher’s identity as a math educator, shaped by lived experiences. Subscale 1 captures the biographical history of the respondent as a math learner — positive vs. negative experiences, positive vs. negative critical memories, and whether they had a favorite math teacher.

Response format: Four-point forced-choice Likert — Strongly Disagree (1), Disagree (2), Agree (3), Strongly Agree (4). No neutral midpoint.

Keying: “+” = agreement indicates a more positive learner experience (high on the construct). All ten items below are keyed positive as drafted.

Table of specifications (this subscale): Receiving 1, Responding 2, Valuing 3, Organization 2, Characterization 2 = 10 (Krathwohl, Bloom, & Masia, 1964).

Items

#Affective LevelItemKeyGrounding
1ReceivingWhen I was in school, I was willing to pay attention to math rather than tune it out.+Willingness to receive/attend (Krathwohl). Boaler: dis/engagement from math.
2RespondingAs a math student, I willingly took part in class activities, such as answering questions or presenting my work.+Willingness to respond. Boaler: participation, reasoning aloud.
3RespondingWhen I worked through a math problem in school, I felt a sense of satisfaction.+Satisfaction in response. Boaler: joy/satisfaction in math thinking.
4ValuingLooking back, I had at least one math teacher I genuinely liked or admired.+Acceptance of a value by a person. Construct def.: favorite math teacher.
5ValuingWhen math got hard, I was willing to keep working at it rather than give up.+Preference/commitment to a value. Boaler: struggle and mistakes as growth.
6ValuingOne of my better memories from school is a time I succeeded at something in math.+A valued critical memory. Construct def.: positive critical memories.
7OrganizationCompared with my other school subjects, math was one I cared most about.+Organizing a value system (relative priority).
8OrganizationMy positive experiences in math became part of how I saw myself as a capable learner.+Integrating the value into a self-system.
9CharacterizationBy the time I finished school, thinking of myself as someone who is “good at math” felt natural.+Settled, automatic self-view. Boaler: the “math person” belief.
10CharacterizationLooking back on my whole time as a student, my relationship with math was a positive one.+Global, summative self-characterization (subscale anchor).

Design notes

1. Valence and taxonomy level are orthogonal. Krathwohl’s levels index depth of internalization, not whether an experience was positive or negative — a respondent can be deeply “characterized” by a negative math identity. Treat the taxonomy as a content-sampling blueprint (it guarantees that items span from shallow attention to deep identity), while the Likert keying carries valence. State this explicitly so the specification table is not read as a scoring ladder.

2. Reverse-keying. Ten same-direction items invite yea-saying. Piloting roughly three reverse-worded items (two candidates above) is the standard hedge and ties to the response-bias discussion in Bandalos (2018), Ch. 13.

3. Retrospective register. Items 7–10 ask respondents to reconstruct a past self-concept; current identity and recall bias can color those reports — the same temporal-confound concern flagged via Simpson’s Paradox. A limitation to name, not a flaw to fix.

Sources: Willis et al. (2023), ToMI; Boaler, Mathematical Mindsets (2022) and “Pedagogical Variety” (2025); Bandalos (2018). Working draft — wording not yet finalized.

Subscale 2 — Perceptions of What Math Is

Math Teacher Identity Instrument

Subscale 2 — Perception of What Math Is: Item Bank

Construct: How the respondent conceives of mathematics, sampled across the facets of the working definition (reasoning, creativity, connection-making, interpretation of methods, plus the descriptors that math illuminates the world and keeps changing). High: math as more than calculation. Low: math as only calculation with one right answer.

Format — bipolar forced choice: each item presents two anchored statements; the respondent picks which is closer and how strongly. Four points, no neutral midpoint (satisfies the forced-choice requirement by construction):

Almost entirely A · Mostly to A · Mostly B · Almost entirely B

Keyed high: the “→” column marks which pole counts as high on the construct. The keyed-high pole alternates between A and B across items, on purpose — see Design Note 2.

Table of specifications (this subscale): Receiving 2, Responding 1, Valuing 2, Organization 2, Characterization 3 = 10 (Krathwohl, Bloom, & Masia, 1964).

Design notes

1. Why bipolar forced-choice answers the social-pressure concern. Decomposing the definition into facets improves content coverage and variance, but on its own it does not relieve social desirability — and badging the components as Boaler’s definition can intensify it by signaling the ‘correct’ answer. Two moves do the real work: (a) keep the expert attribution out of what the respondent sees (neutral stems, no name attached), and (b) write the traditional pole as a position a thoughtful teacher could actually hold, not a strawman. Bipolar forced choice does both at once and removes the neutral midpoint by construction.

2. The keyed-high pole alternates between A and B, by design. If the reform side were always B, respondents would detect the pattern within a few items and straight-line it — reintroducing the very pressure we are removing. Alternating direction (here: B, A, B, A, B, B, A, B, A, B) breaks the response set and forces each item to be read on its merits.

3. One response format across the subscale (refines the earlier plan). The two Receiving and one Responding items are written as bipolar contrasts too, so the whole subscale uses a single format. Mixing agreement-Likert and bipolar within one subscale would add method variance that inflates inter-item correlations independent of the construct. If I end up preferring the earlier mix, the three R/R items can be reverted easily.

4. Cognitive interviewing is non-negotiable for bipolar items. Confirm that respondents read A and B as opposite ends of one dimension rather than two separate claims they might both endorse or both reject and keep each pole to a single idea so it does not become double-barreled.

5. Pilot the two formats against each other. Run the bipolar form and the agreement form with overlapping samples, then compare endorsement spread, ceiling effects, and how each relates to a short social-desirability check. Keep whichever shows more variance and weaker desirability coupling. Ties to the response-bias discussion in Bandalos (2018), Ch. 13.

Sources: Boaler, Mathematical Mindsets (2022) and “Pedagogical Variety” (2025); Willis et al. (2023), ToMI; Bandalos (2018). Working draft — wording not yet finalized; respondent-facing version should omit the level/facet and keying columns.

Subscale 3 — Pedagogy and Math Identity

Math Teacher Identity Instrument

Subscale 3 — Pedagogy and Math Identity: Item Bank

Construct: The respondent’s beliefs about math teaching and about students, and their identity as a math educator. High: authentic curriculum, students seen as capable, no deficit mindset, teacher as a continuous learner who also learns from students. Low: the ‘banking’/transmission model, teacher as sole authority, one correct answer, passive students, rigid instruction and grading.

Response format: Four-point forced-choice Likert — Strongly Disagree (1), Disagree (2), Agree (3), Strongly Agree (4). No neutral midpoint.

Keying: “+” = agreement indicates high on the construct; “−” = reverse-scored (agreement indicates low). This subscale carries three reverse-keyed items (≈ 30%).

Table of specifications (this subscale): Receiving 1, Responding 2, Valuing 2, Organization 2, Characterization 3 = 10 (Krathwohl, Bloom, & Masia, 1964).

Items

#Affective LevelItemKeyGrounding
1ReceivingI pay attention to the different ways my students reason through a math problem, not just whether they get it right.+Attending to student thinking.
2RespondingWhen a student makes a mistake in math, I treat it as a useful opportunity for learning.+Boaler: mistakes as brain growth.
3RespondingI actively invite students to share and defend their own strategies in class.+Boaler: reasoning aloud, ‘convince a skeptic.’
4ValuingI believe every student is capable of learning mathematics to a high level.+Asset-based; rejects the ‘math gift’ myth (Boaler).
5ValuingSome students simply aren’t cut out for higher-level math.−Deficit mindset (reverse). Boaler debunks the gift belief.
6OrganizationI draw on a variety of teaching approaches because different ones open up different kinds of mathematical learning.+Pedagogical variety; Boaler’s anti-doctrine point.
7OrganizationGood math teaching is mostly about clearly explaining the right procedures.−Transmission stance (reverse). The ‘banking’ model.
8CharacterizationI see myself as someone who keeps learning mathematics alongside my students.+Continuous learner who learns from students.
9CharacterizationIn my classroom, students’ main role is to absorb the math I present.−Passive students / sole authority (reverse).
10CharacterizationBeing a math teacher who helps every student see themselves as capable is central to who I am.+Identity-level, asset-based.

Design notes

1. Valence and taxonomy level are orthogonal. As with the other subscales, the levels sample depth of internalization, while the keying (three reverse items) carries the high/low direction. A teacher can be strongly characterized by a transmission identity.

2. Social desirability is the strongest threat on this subscale. Methods-program students know the asset-based, growth-mindset ‘right’ answers, so straight self-report can ceiling out. Reverse-keyed transmission/deficit items are the main defense; consider whether a brief social-desirability check or behaviorally specific wording is warranted. See Bandalos (2018), Ch. 13.

3. ‘Identity’ here is current and aspirational. Several items ask who the respondent is as a teacher; for pre-service teachers with little classroom experience, these are partly aspirational rather than enacted. Worth naming, and worth a behavioral-anchor pass later if the gap matters.

4. Double-barrel watch. Items 1 and 7 contain a contrast clause (‘not just…’, ‘mostly about…’). They read as single stances, but flag for cognitive interviewing in case respondents split on the two halves.
