Research proposal

Abstract

In this proposal, I lay out a study based on the Youth Participatory Action Research (YPAR) epistemology, framework, and methodology to study the interaction of mathematics education and technology within a critical race perspective. Mathematics in the Western education context is often still considered completely immutable, objective, and True and this mindset can be incredibly dehumanizing to students as they are being required to learn something that is explicitly denying their knowledge and life experiences. The nature of YPAR demands that specific research questions, tools, methodologies, and implementations be designed within the context of the community selected. My target community will be a middle or high school math classroom within the greater Omaha area.

Introduction and Rationale

The topic of this research proposal is how YPAR might be used to disrupt and transform traditional mathematics education, specifically within the context of technology access. The statement made by Stavrou and Miller (2017) that the “Western worldview regards mathematical knowledge as being uncontestable, objective, and dissociated from experience, history, and cultures” (p. 95) lays bare the issues education still struggles with relating to race, equity, technology, and mathematics. My goal with this study is to propose a new way of looking at and teaching mathematics and incorporating technology that is not only more inclusive to all students but also helps students challenge the dominant order to foment change beyond the classroom.

Throughout my short teaching career, I have noticed that students are disengaged from math content. Originally, my thoughts were that this is often due to them lacking a deep understanding of numeracy concepts and instead relying on a set of procedures to solve problems without truly understanding what they are doing. This leads to a fear of failure and, as a result, students disengage from the content. However, as I have developed as an educator and researcher, I have come to understand that this disengagement and so called “achievement gap” is a larger systemic issue that is the result of the civilizational and institutional racism that is built into the foundation of this country.

Youth participatory action research presents an entirely (to the Western world) way of teaching, researching, and knowing that centers the students as the experts and leverages their individual, family, and cultural experiences to shape the learning taking place within a math classroom. This has the potential to make the math classroom a place where significantly more authentic learning takes place and students become invested in their learning in a way that benefits them personally, and not just test scores reported to the state.

Background Literature

Conceptual Framework

Cammarota et al. (2017) define and summarize YPAR in the paragraph below cited from page three of their Youth Community Leaders curriculum:

“Youth participatory action research (YPAR) is a pedagogical process that cultivates the recognition of oppression through reflection and action or what Brazilian scholar and philosopher Paulo Freire calls ‘praxis.’ It is through praxis that young people of color transform their perspective into critical consciousness. Linking praxis to YPAR suggests that research and pedagogical objectives center on challenging and overcoming an unjust social order. Through reflection and action, youth illuminate how societal institutions, such as schools, can shift from sites of oppression to spaces of liberation.  They look to challenge dominant representations of their identity and culture attempting to perpetuate their subordination. The challenge leads to new visions of education that foster their voices, community commitments, and social activism.”

YPAR is an approach that can work to re-humanize and de-colonialize both the mathematics classroom but also educational research.

Literature Review

Introduction

No one wants to be the passive receiver of what someone else decides is best for them so why would we think that “educating” students that way would be effective? Tanner’s (2015) describes YPAR as “a radical shift from traditional education practices because it relies on the following pedagogical assumptions: (1) youth should design their own outcomes in collaboration with adults, (2) power should be shared between the youth collective and facilitator, and (3) the ideas the collective generates dictate the research agenda and curriculum” (p. 65). YPAR presents a new way of teaching, learning, and knowing that actively and authentically involves students as stakeholders in their learning and works towards disrupting the dominant powers that currently work to oppress and marginalize communities that do not fit the “cultural ideal.”

YPAR and Counternarratives as Math Curriculum

Gonzales et al. (2021) state that their research “allowed the non-youth authors to center the narratives and experiences of students as an invaluable expertise” (p. 74) and go on to say that because of this they write as a “collective us.” YPAR throws out the traditional banking model of education and instead challenges educators to take a “step back and support the creative genius naturally ingrained in youth” (Gonzalez et al, 2017, p. 82). This is especially important in the field of mathematics because it is often considered completely immutable, objective, and True and this mindset can be incredibly dehumanizing to students as they are being required to learn something that is explicitly denying their knowledge and life experiences. Martinez and Yeong I (2019) assert that for a student to be successful in mathematics they must “see themselves as capable and powerful mathematical learners when participating in mathematics in personal and social ways” (p. 252) and go on to suggest that YPAR is a highly effective method of creating a learning environment where this outcome is possible.

Yeh et al. (2021) go on to say that for YPAR to be effective we as mathematics educators need to “navigate complex terrain and search beyond privileged spaces to locate knowledge from communities that disrupt colonial systems of knowledge making” (p. 73). Both Martinez and I (2019) and Yeh et al. (2021) discuss how mathematics treats identity formation as an individual activity, but it is also shaped and supported (or not) by our families, cultures, and their collective experiences. Specifically, Yeh et al. (2021) reference Anzaldua’s concept of borderlands consciousness and say that acknowledging, supporting, and centering this type of knowing can “empower educators and students of color in that they recognize the advantage of having insights into the experiences of the Oppressed and the Oppressors and may reveal the cracks that support social change” (p. 81).

Torre and Ayala (2009) call this type of learning and knowing “theoriz[ing] from the flesh” (p. 387) and also reference Anzaldua’s concept of Borderlands consciousness in their work towards creating liberatory YPAR within the classroom. Particularly impactful is their statement that “challenging traditional notions of expertise, PAR assumes that everyday people carry deep knowledge and analysis about the conditions of their lives and should lead in determining meaningful research questions, designs, methods, analyses and products” (p. 388). YPAR is an inherently disruptive epistemology and pedagogy in that it removes the researcher and/or teacher as “expert” and instead recenters the voices, stories, and knowledge of those who are typically considered passive objects to be studied or taught. For YPAR to be successful the participants must be from the stakeholders within the community, school, or classroom and a community of safety, mutual respect, and collaboration must already be in place. Without these things, true YPAR is not possible.

Mathematics and Technology

Technology has become ever-present in our world, and especially after the COVID-19 pandemic, technology has become a staple in K-12 classrooms. The implementation of and access to this technology has been somewhat patchwork across the country and varies between districts, schools, and often even by classroom. This patchwork implementation and access is a serious equity issue is a “central concern when considering the effective teaching and learning of mathematics” (Ruef et al., 2022, p. 1). Ruef et al. (2022) also pointed out that COVID-19 exposed and exacerbated inequities seen with specifically online learning and that “students were challenged in finding new ways of connecting in online class settings, and that opportunities to learn were mediated by access to, and fluency with, technology” (p. 2). The primary focus of their research was how teachers and students establish and sustained caring and supportive relationships with online education, and as mentioned above, such relationships are crucial for true YPAR to take place within the classroom. Interestingly, one of the largest challenges the author’s discovered was the dearth of effective digital, technological tools stating, “despite advances in graphing software, mathematics education retains its focus on pencil-and-paper activities” (Ruef et al., 2021, p. 5). Eppard et al. (2022) looked specifically at iPad integration into the math classroom and stated that “the iPad transforms students’ learning experience by providing instant access to important educational resources anywhere and anytime” (p. 374). They go on to suggest that the use of iPads in the math classroom supports student-centered learning because “the tablet devices provide students with a more interactive and collaborative learning experience” (p. 375). However, the students in this study were all Indian nationals living in Dubai and attending a private school for Indian nationals. This is not a particularly representative population and the authors themselves acknowledge that the use of iPads removes most collaboration within the classroom and that that is not an ideal learning environment for many students.

Rubin (1999) wrote that “rather than looking at math education from the perspective of the computer, we must look at computers from the perspective of mathematics education” and continues saying, “the role of technology in the math classroom must be in service of goals we hold for students’ mathematical knowledge and expertise” (p. 1). Feng et al. (2023) in their report on the impact of technology-supported learning on long-term math learning echoes Rubin’s statements from nearly 25 years ago with the finding that technology-based programs have significant potential to improve math learning for all students. They offer the ever-important caveat to this finding by cautioning that technology-based programs must be supplemental and “aligned to class instruction” (p. 17). Unlike Eppard et al., Feng et al. conducted their research in diverse and primarily urban schools, but they solicited no input or feedback from the students or parents, which Eppard et al. did collect to some degree in their research. What is clear from this small selection of research is that mathematics research is still quite centered around traditional Western epistemologies, and the research participants are passive objects to be studied, measured, and acted upon. Stavrou and Miller (2017) argue for more de-colonial research methods within the field of mathematics with the statement that “applying decolonizing strategies to mathematics will make visible that is a subject developed in time and place and will expose the imperialistic operation that deprives mathematics of its historical roots and human construction” (p. 106). Technology has the potential to be effective tool in working to decolonize mathematics education, but that will never be the case with the current research and deficit mindsets surrounding the use of technology in the classroom.

Mathematics and Student Motivation

Abu and Kribushi (2022) attempt to bridge the gap between the perceived lack of student motivation surrounding math utilizing technology after observing that, “the use of technology has not led to a revolution in teaching or learning” (p. 3). In their study they used a digital writing board as a method of potentially increasing student motivation in the math classroom. While the study found some increase in student self-reported motivation, they did note that student achievement scores did significantly increase as a result of using the digital writing board. Their conclusion was that the “use of [a] digital board affects the learning climate” (Abu and Kribushi, 2022, p. 9). This again points to the effectiveness of the principles of YPAR, because it was not so much the technology that increased student motivation and academic achievement, but its appropriate implementation creating a more positive and collaborative learning environment. Alrajeh and Shindel (2020) further support this theme by stating that “instructional support was the most dominant factor [in] determining student engagement” (p. 177).

Light and Pierson (2014) specifically looked at Khan Academy as a technological instructional support that might increase student engagement. Khan Academy is a gamification model of mathematical learning and gamification is intended to be the external motivating factor to increase student motivation and engagement with mathematical learning. According to Light and Pierson (2014), “educational policy makers around the globe often talk about Khan Academy with great expectations for deeply transforming teaching and learning, with the concept of ‘flipping the classroom’ where students receive direct instruction via video outside of the classroom and then work with the teacher doing math in the classroom” (p. 117). They go on to point out how this use of technology is a large equity issue as it assumes a level of access to technology and connectivity that many families do not have access to or can afford. Their findings were that “teachers embedding Khan Academy resources within their traditional instructional practices and creating a decidedly non-traditional—yet improved—learning environment” had the most practical and positive outcomes (p. 117).

Finally, Niemi et al. (2018) incorporated digital storytelling (DST) into the math classroom in an attempt to increase student engagement and math literacy. Niemi et al. (2018) chose a collaborative model of DST as they felt that “learning through DST is a socially and culturally related process that occurs when there are interactions among learners, material tools, psychological tools, and other people” (p. 333). Their findings indicated that DST is an effective “pedagogical means for using digital technology to motivate students to become active learners” (p. 347). This study took place within two distinctly different educational contexts and cultures (Chinese and Finnish) and the results were similar in both contexts. This study is also one of the few that comes close to approximating anything even related to YPAR. The implication here is that a collaborative learning environment focused on students’ funds of knowledge and using that knowledge for change is one of the most effective ways to cultivate student motivation and engagement in the classroom.

Conclusion

Overall, the state of mathematics educational research is still very quantitative in nature, and “data” focused. However, as Martin et al. (2017) have noted there is beginnings of a “sociopolitical turn [emphasis original] in mathematics education” and there is an “increased attention to the political aspects of mathematics education, including knowledge, power, and identity” (p. 607). Youth Participatory Action Research has started to gain a foothold in mathematics education and shows promise as both an epistemology and ontology that can transform mathematics education. Stavrou and Miller (2017) sum it up best by saying, “we believe that if researchers and educators acknowledge the root causes of inequality, then there would be a shift in the literature away from positing a lack of cultural relevance as the reason that Indigenous students are disengaged with mathematics” (p. 110). While their research focused on Aboriginal and Indigenous students, this statement applies to all students learning mathematics.

Methodology

Research Questions

The nature of YPAR necessitates that research questions be designed and written with the input of the stakeholders participating. Thus, there is no formal research question created at the start of the project, only a general area of interest to study, with the understanding that this may also change based on the needs and interests of the participants. My area of interest within this study is to use YPAR to study the intersection of mathematics education, technology, and race.

Research Approach

As displayed in Figure 1, YPAR is a cyclical process and is directed by the participants themselves. This means that much of the predetermined research approach will be focused on building trust and community within the chosen classroom. This will be accomplished by being present in the classroom as an active participant prior to the research taking place, providing education to the students and their parents about what the YPAR research will entail, and helping students develop the research questions that will guide all further research.

Researcher’s Statement

The following subsection is adapted from my “Personal Curriculum Platform” essay from EDU 601 Critical Issues at Doane University:

“In my view, the overarching purpose of education is to create lifelong learners equipped with the tools to navigate the complexities of our society. Students want to learn, and it is the teacher’s job to ensure that students are afforded the opportunity to do so. This is done by providing a safe, equitable environment where students are appropriately challenged with relevant content. The issue then becomes defining what safe, equitable, opportunity, and challenge mean in the context of education. For education to be all those things at a global level, universal definitions must be agreed upon.

Safety in the context of education is much more than physical safety, although that is a part of it. Students living in fear of an active shooter because our legislators are unwilling to pass common-sense gun legislation means that they cannot devote all their attention to learning. Similarly, if the culture of the school is not safe, whether that be overt favoritism of certain groups or the more insidious tracking of students and access to classes, students will not feel safe emotionally. To me, for a school to be safe, there must be an inclusive environment that respects and celebrates differences between students. The school must also be active in contributing to its community.

Equity goes hand in hand with safety. An equitable school not only allows all students to access all classes, but it also develops a strong curriculum to ensure students have an equal opportunity to succeed in those classes. Equitable means that teachers and students are treated with respect regardless of race, ethnicity, orientation, or any other defining characteristic. Schools must be active in ensuring all their students are reflected in the curriculum as well.

To appropriately challenge students with relevant content, the curriculum must not be a static monolith but, instead, a living thing that is changed and adapted for each student. Gone are the days of assuming that all students enter the classroom at the same place, learn at the same pace, or exit the curriculum at the same time as their peers. As Wiggins so eloquently says: [we need to] ‘make education modern: forward-looking, client-centered, and flexible; adapted to an era where the future, not the past, determines the curriculum’ (Wiggins, 2015, p. 229).

In my classroom, I work toward these goals by creating trusting, reciprocal relationships of students to teacher and student to student. If there is no trust in the classroom, then it is not possible for learning to take place. Because I only have direct experience with my cultural background, it is my responsibility to educate myself on the cultural backgrounds of my students. This is done by individual student conferences, ‘get-to-know-you’ activities throughout the year, and follow-up research on my own. It is not the job of my students to educate me about their culture, but they can provide me with a starting point to access understanding of the cultures.

I hesitate at the idea of classifying a student as a ‘type’ of learner (e.g. kinesthetic, auditory, or visual) because depending on the subject or the day, a student may fall into one or more different categories. As our understanding of the brain has increased, the view of your brain being a static thing after you reach your adult years has shifted to the idea that a brain is highly plastic. One of Zadina’s main arguments in her book is that ‘learning is rewiring’ (Zadina, 2014, p. 15). A student has successfully learned when they can reuse the pathway developed in the brain to retrieve the information imparted to them. Repeated experience is crucial to learning and a student is more likely to learn something if they are able to tie it to something in their own life (‘background knowledge’)” (S. Seim, personal communication, April 9, 2021).

Context

As I am not currently teaching, I will need to partner with a local district, school, and teacher to access a classroom. Given my connections to the Omaha metro area, the most likely districts I would work with are Ralston Public Schools, Bellevue Public Schools, or Omaha Public Schools. I have the most connection to Ralston Public Schools so some basic information about that district is provided below. Ralston Public Schools is a small district surrounded by the larger district of Omaha Public Schools. The student population of Omaha is 16 times the size of Ralston’s. The city of Ralston is landlocked by Omaha, and due to shifting city limits, several of the elementary schools within the Ralston Public Schools district lie in the city of Omaha proper while serving residents of Ralston.

Ralston specializes in educating students who have significant hearing loss up to total loss. Students from all over the Omaha metropolitan area are sent by their home districts to the Ralston school district. As a result, the percentage of students with hearing disabilities in the Ralston district is disproportionately higher than any other district in the Omaha metropolitan area. Ralston Public Schools also has a large immigrant population. Because Ralston is a smaller district nestled within Omaha city limits, roughly 33% of the population are students who opt into the district. Just over 50% of the student population qualifies for free and reduced lunch. The English language learner (EL) population in RPS makes up roughly 7% of the student population. The graduation rate at RHS is 84.41% for the 2020 school year which is slightly lower than the state average. As I do not know what school or classroom I might be in, I am not able to provide more specific demographics for potential stakeholders within the research study.

Similarly, since I do not have a specific classroom and the nature of YPAR research is that timelines, specific research questions, and methodologies cannot be determined until stakeholders have been identified and agreed to participate, I have only basic questions and methodologies that I might prefer but I am well aware that these will almost certainly change as the project progresses.

Methods of Investigation

Assuming that these are the data sources chosen by the stakeholders for the research project, products from counter-storytelling and Pláticas will be coded by both the researchers and stakeholders for common themes and threads. Reference checking will take place with the stakeholders by having them review the coding and themes developed to ensure that themes are accurate, and no important themes are missed by the researchers. If qualitative tools such as a survey are used, data will be analyzed looking for trends in data that align or are misaligned with the data from the counter-stories and Pláticas. Above all else, the voices, stories, and experiences of the stakeholders will be considered the primary source of data regardless of how it is gathered and analyzed.

Anticipated Products

Over the course of this study, I hope to develop a fuller picture of the intersection of mathematics education, technology, and racism in the upper-level, public school classroom. I would like to determine several recommendations and actionable practices that can be shared to more general audiences for implantation throughout the country. From my research and reading, students need to be active participants in their learning and developing their curriculum. The results of this study will not only inform any future potential teaching practices but more immediately inform the direction of future research as I will have a clearer picture of how youth participatory action research changes the dynamic of a classroom and learning that takes place.

The results of this specific study may have limited generalizability as each classroom, school, district, and community faces varying challenges and obstacles differently, even if they are facing the “same” challenges and obstacles as another community. However, the concepts of implementing YPAR in this study are generalizable across communities as the goal of YPAR is to provide a framework that is stakeholder driven and focused on actionable solutions for the specific community in question.
