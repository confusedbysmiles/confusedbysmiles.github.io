TEAC 924J

Learning Reflection #2

Course reflection

In the second half of our class, the focus shifted towards the intersection of race, ethnicity, and culture. With a special focus on multilingual learners and indigenous cultures. Since I am a former math teacher, my reading selections were more math focused and thus this reflection will discuss connections and reflections within the field of mathematics rather than STEM as a whole. Martin et al. (2017) make the observation that the field of mathematics is slowly catching up to other educational research as there has been a “sociopolitical turn in mathematics education” which is a “growing movement characterized by increased attention to the political aspects of mathematics education, including knowledge, power, and identity” (p. 607). As we have discussed in class, math is one of the last holdouts in education where many, if not most, educators insist that mathematics as a field is objective and neutral and politics and social justice issues have no place in their classrooms.

Martin et al. (2017) go on to state that “racialized narratives about students’ intellectual and mathematical abilities play a central role in processes of positioning and identification” (p. 613) and as Ladson-Billings (2014) points out in her article on culturally sustaining teaching (CST) our “teacher education courses [are] filled mostly with young White women from suburban (and some rural) communities who still thought about people of color (particularly African American and Latin@ students) in deficit terms” (p. 78). What this means, is that even if we accept that mathematical knowledge is “Objective and True” (which we definitely shouldn’t), we as teachers are bringing race and politics into the classrooms without biases, preconceptions, and assumptions about what our students do (or do not) know and what they are (or are not) capable of. This is hugely problematic if not examined and addressed as we are pigeon-holing students not based on their actual ability and knowledge but on our own biases and assumptions. We are doing the opposite of what Ladson-Billings (2014) challenges us to do with true CST by “ensuring that consistently marginalized students are repositioned into a place of normativity–that is, they become subjects in the instructional process, not mere objects” (p. 76).

Grapin et al. (2023) proposed a conceptual framework for justice-centered STEM education that focused on three tenants they felt would coincide with and support CST in the STEM classroom, especially for multilingual learners (MLs). Those three tenants are:

  1. “Justice-centered STEM education engages students in pressing societal challenges” (p. 524)

  2. “Given the complexity of pressing societal challenges, justice-centered STEM education leverages the convergence of STEM subjects, such as data science and computer science, to explain these challenges” (p. 524).

  3. “Justice-centered STEM education engages students in designing justice-centered solutions that center the knowledge and experiences of minoritized students” (p. 524).

Grapin et al. (2023) go on to state that MLs are often doubly minoritized for not only linguistically but because they are often also members of typically minoritized racial and/or ethnic groups (p. 525). In the math classroom this can be further compounded because math is functionally its own language that is being taught in English, and MLs are now trying to learn two new languages at once and only have access to mathematical language through English. It is no wonder we often see these students struggle in this type of model! The goal of justice-centered STEM education according to Grapin et al. (2017) is to “shift attention from perceived deficits of individuals (e.g., people are not trying hard enough to avoid COVID-19) to the policies and practices of societal systems that have privileged some groups while marginalizing others (e.g., lack of access to affordable housing in the housing system)” (p. 538).

Esmonde and Caswell (2010) take Grapin et al.’s (2023) idea of transnational funds of knowledge and explicitly state that “all students are capable of dealing with complex ideas” and that we as teachers are responsible for “providing opportunities for students to learn academically challenging knowledge and skills, and fostering a shared sense of responsibility for learning within collaborative groupings” (p. 250). They also state that while “academic excellence is taken as necessary” it is “not sufficient, for equity” (p. 245). This point stood out to me because with the ideal of American individualism and meritocracy, we often forget that “just doing well in school” is not enough to ensure that students have equitable access to the dominant culture and resources around them. Yosso (2005) outlines six types of cultural capital that they state forms community cultural wealth and can allow typically minoritized groups to access and contribute to the dominant culture capital. Those six categories are listed below (p. 77 - 80, emphasis in the original):

  1. Aspirational capital refers to the ability to maintain hopes and dreams for the future, even in the face of real and perceived barriers.

  2. Linguistic capital includes the intellectual and social skills attained through communication experiences in more than one language and/or style.

  3. Familial capital refers to those cultural knowledges nurtured among familia (kin) that carry a sense of community, history, memory and cultural intuition.

  4. Social capital can be understood as networks of people and community resources.

  5. Navigational capital refers to the skills of maneuvering through social institutions.

  6. Resistant capital refers [to] those knowledges and skills fostered through oppositional behavior that challenges inequality.

Ensuring that we as educators are aware of these non-Western funds of knowledge and making space for them within our classroom is critically important in creating a classroom truly based on the CST and critical race frameworks. It is also important to note that these funds of knowledge can help all students learn, not only the mathematical content being taught in the classroom, but how to be a more inclusive, aware, and reflective person in general. It is also important to point out that from the research of Barwell et al. (2017) comes the finding that shows “that bilingual learners face difficulties quite similar to those documented for native speakers” (p. 589). This finding is really quite earth shattering if one pauses to take in the repercussions. We often sequester or track students in math based on their language ability, and this finding from Barwell et al. points to the fact that our current method of tracking students based on their linguistic abilities may be more about the dominant culture’s systemic racism and deficit mindset rather than actual science and good teaching. Just sit with that thought for a moment and ask yourself where else that might be true.

With this rather sobering realization, let us turn to the research from Stavrou and Miller (2017) that focuses specifically on the intersection and friction between Western math and aboriginal cultures. Stavrou and Miller (2017) assert that by “applying decolonizing strategies to mathematics will make visible that it is a subject developed in time and place, and will expose the imperialistic operation that deprives mathematics of its historical roots and human construction” (p. 106). The authors go on to challenge educators who work to bring non-Western cultures into the classroom to make sure they are not sanitizing the history in the process and cite the following example “although residential schools violently forced Aboriginal peoples to forsake and forget their languages, cultures, and identities, educationalist now want Aboriginal peoples to recall and share their cultures and traditions for the purpose of multiculturalism in the classroom and what they presume will lead to Aboriginal success in ‘hard’ subjects” (p. 106). They continue on reminding us that while we describe Aboriginal peoples “as being reckless and irresponsible for losing their cultures, as opposed to the acknowledgement that their languages were ‘slayed’ and their cultures subject to destruction through violence, legislation, and policy decisions, as well as shaming” (p. 106).

All throughout these readings, and even somewhat in our class discussions, I kept coming back to a note a wrote in the margins of the Grapin et al. article that says “while I think their intent is to provide MLs with agency in their learning, the authors; language feels exploitative and takes the tone of ‘What can MLs do for STEM?’” This led me back to a closing quote from Stavrou and Miller (2017) where they said they “believe that if researchers and educators acknowledge the root causes of inequality, then there would be a shift in the literature away from positing a lack of cultural relevance as the reason that Indigenous students are disengaged with mathematics” (p. 110). How do we as STEM educational leaders help lead this shift when Matias (2016) points out that “the current climate of teacher education renders attachment to socially just ideals as fashionable as skinny jeans, complete with promises to ‘close the achievement gap’ and ‘save’ urban schools” but we “rarely interrogate the question of why an achievement gap exists in the first place” (p. 194).
