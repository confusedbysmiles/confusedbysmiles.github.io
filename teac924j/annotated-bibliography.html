Annotated bibliography

Adelman, M., Nonnenmacher, S., Borman, B., & Kosciw, J. G. (2022). Gen Z GSAs: Trans-Affirming and Racially Inclusive Gender-Sexuality Alliances in Secondary Schools. Teachers College Record, 124(8), 192-219. https://doi-org.libproxy.unl.edu/10.1177/01614681221123129

Type: Qualitative (Critical pragmatism)

The purpose of this study was to examine the formation of a student’s identity within the LGBTQ+ community using Gender-Sexuality Alliance (GSA) clubs as a form of support and community. There were 20 participants, 10 cisgender students and 10 trans or nonbinary students between the ages of 14 and 18 who were participating in their high school GSA programs. This study used a critical pragmatism approach to analyze the contents of their interviews with the students. The researchers found that GSA clubs can be a space “for marginalized LBGTQ students to create a collective empowering identity, they can also be a space where some differences may be flattened or left out” (p. 193). This type of identity development and formation is important because as the researchers point out “our findings demonstrate how GSA students leverage their identity as a goal when mobilizing themselves and their peers to alter a school’s norms and practices” (p. 193).

This type of work is incredibly important as prior research has shown the mere existence of a GSA club in a school is beneficial to the mental wellbeing of LGBTQ+ students and the specific focus of this article is the intersection of race and queer identities and how the formation of this type of collective identity creates both groups of students and individuals who are more comfortable in their identities. This provides more opportunities for these students to advocate and push for meaningful changes not just in their schools, but in their larger communities.

Cicero, E. C., & Wesp, L. M. (2017). Supporting the Health and Well-Being of Transgender Students. Journal of School Nursing, 33(2), 95-108. https://doi-org.libproxy.unl.edu/10.1177/1059840516689705

Type: Not Primary Research

While this article is not primary research, it contains several excellent definitions to commonly misunderstood words and contains suggestions for how school nurses can support transgender students that are readily generalizable to all staff within a school. The following definitions from pages 2 and 3 will be used within my presentation:

  • Transgender - “an umbrella term inclusive of diverse communities of people. Transgender, or trans, describes an individual when their sex assigned at birth does not correspond to their gender expression or gender identity.

  • Gender Identity - an individual’s sense of being male, female, both, or neither.

  • Gender expression - describes a person’s physical appearance, clothing choice, and behaviors used to express aspects of gender identity or role.

  • Cisgender - an individual’s gender identity and sex at birth align.

    • Note: The term cisgender should be used versus saying that an individual is not transgender.

  • Sex assignment or natal sex - is determined at birth based on external genitalia, and sometimes genetics (often categorized as male, female, or intersex).

  • Gender nonconforming - a person with a gender expression that is at odds with cultural gender expressions.

  • The article concludes that school nurses “play a key role in creating a space that is welcoming and affirming where transgender students can thrive” (p. 12). School nurses can do so by providing gender affirming spaces and educating other staff, who in turn, with education can and should emulate.

Forbes, T. D. (2022). Queer-Free Majors?: LGBTQ+ College Students' Accounts of Chilly and Warm Academic Disciplines. Journal of LGBT Youth, 19(3), 330-349. https://doi-org.libproxy.unl.edu/10.1080/19361653.2020.1813673

Type: Qualitative (Interview)

This study focuses on queer students who have enrolled in college and their struggles to navigate the typically heterosexual spaces within higher education with their identity as a queer person. The authors used interview data from 20 college students to “describe the process by which respondents constructed some majors or disciplines, especially STEM fields, as queer-free and others, especially the social sciences, as queer-friendly” (p. 330). These interviews and discussions point out how academic tracking of queer students into “queer-friendly” majors continues to perpetuate inequalities both in college and beyond. Based on the interview data, campus climate is trending in a positive direction, however, queer students continue to report experiencing microaggressions and verbal threats. This leads to tracking of queer students, often self-imposed, towards majors in the social sciences and liberal arts fields. The authors point out that this is problematic because “the common conceptualization of the university’s role is to prepare students for the labor force, but evidence suggests that some queer students are learning to limit their expectations of their future careers, perhaps in light of previous and anticipated future discrimination” (p. 333). This is essentially stunting a student’s identity and causing them to shed or hide parts of themselves to conform to what they perceive as societal expectations and, in the process, can limit their potential to the careers they have access to and as well as constrict their earning potential.

Garvey, J. C. (2019). Queer Quantitative Query: Sexual Orientation in Higher Education Surveys. Journal of College Student Development, 60(4), 495-501. http://libproxy.unl.edu/login?url=https://www-proquest-com.libproxy.unl.edu/scholarly-journals/queer-quantitative-query-sexual-orientation/docview/2461130097/se-2

Type: Quantitative (Survey)

This article focuses on how “the dearth of quantitative research on sexual orientation in higher education is highly problematic yet not entirely surprising, particularly considering the historical exclusion of sexual orientation in national higher education data sets” (p. 495). The purpose of this study is to determine “how is sexual orientation demographic information operationalized and collected in prevalent higher education quantitative surveys?” (p. 495). This type of research is important in understanding queer students, their identities, and their experiences within the higher education system, because we cannot study what we do not measure, and making sure that the data collected accurately represents the identities of the students we are studying is equally, if not more important, than just having an option to check something other than “straight” or “heterosexual” on a survey. The author in this study chose to collect widely used survey instruments used in higher education and look to see if “(a) whether the survey included a question/item about sexual orientation, and if so, (b) how they question/item was written, paying specific attention to question stems, response options, and formatting” (p. 496). Shockingly, none of the U.S. federal government surveys had an option regarding a participant’s sexual orientation/identity and among nonprofit educational organization surveys, only 60% had an option concerning a participant’s sexual identity. The comprehensiveness of those surveys that did include an option concerning identity were often very limited or used outdated and/or problematic terminology.

Kersey, E., & Voigt, M. (2021). Finding Community and Overcoming Barriers: Experiences of Queer and Transgender Postsecondary Students in Mathematics and Other STEM Fields. Mathematics Education Research Journal, 33(4), 733-756. https://doi-org.libproxy.unl.edu/10.1007/s13394-020-00356-5

Type: Qualitative (Narrative Inquiry)

This paper conducts two separate narrative inquiry studies, one with postsecondary transgender students and a second with queer undergraduate students both focusing on the idea that STEM fields tend to be less welcoming than the humanities or social sciences to those who hold queer identities. These authors made a point to acknowledge that they rejected the idea of “well-defined static identities” and especially with a person’s identities surrounding the sexual orientation and gender expression are “fluid and can change regularly or over time” (p. 739). The authors specifically call out the field of mathematics saying, “educators need to support students’ identity development through ‘queering’ of mathematics in order to make it less male-centric and to counter the absolutism and binary construction of mathematics” (p. 741). The authors note that STEM is a field in which people who present as women are still taken less seriously than people who present as men. An interesting observation made in relation to this statement was that “for trans women, although presenting as male afforded them more social power (often mitigated by gender transgression oppression), the decrease in social power they experienced when presenting as female was still an improvement because it was not accompanied by gender dysphoria” (p. 749). This is important to note because some trans people are saying they were willing to tolerate increased discrimination to live more authentically with their identities. With this in mind, we need to work towards making STEM fields inclusive of all people, regardless of gender orientation or expression.

Kuff, R. M., Greytak, E. A., & Kosciw, J. G. (2019). Supporting Safe and Healthy Schools for Lesbian, Gay, Bisexual, Transgender, and Queer Students: A National Survey of School Counselors, Social Workers, and Psychologists. Gay, Lesbian and Straight Education Network (GLSEN). Retrieved from ERIC http://libproxy.unl.edu/login?url=https://www-proquest-com.libproxy.unl.edu/reports/supporting-safe-healthy-schools-lesbian-gay/docview/2228677216/se-2

Type: Qualitative (Survey)

This survey is a massive undertaking conducted by GLSEN during May to September of 2013. They collected a sample of 1,741 school mental health professionals (SMHPs) working with students grades 5 through 12 in all 50 states as well as Washington D.C. The results showed that one third of SMHPs felt queer students would feel unsafe in their schools, close to half said that males with nonconforming gender expressions would feel unsafe, and nearly 8 in 10 SMHPs said that “bullying, name-calling, and/or harassment of students were serious problems in their schools” and a further 7 in 10 SMHPs “believed that students were bullied at least sometimes based upon their sexual orientation (73.9%) or gender expression (70.4%)” (p. xvii). It is concerning that SMHPs say there is such a problem with bullying based on a student’s gender identity, but they do not seem to extend this same concern to a student’s feeling of safety within the school. This may be in large part due to SMHPs lack of preparation in their school programs for working with queer students and the lack of continuing education pursued or received by SMHPs. The survey points that “about 6 in 10 SMHPs reported never or rarely participating in LGBTQ-specific: in-service training (61.9%); conference workshops and panels (59.4%); or in other trainings and webinars (61.9%). This is in stark contrast to the over 80 percent of SMHPs who reported participating in similar type of professional development on bullying” (p. 24). This type of work is important because it sheds light on the disconnect between the purported support of queer youth and lack of meaningful actionable support and follow through.

Leyva, L. A., McNeill, R. T., Balmer, B. R., Marshall, B. L., King, V. E., & Alley, Z. D. (2022). Black Queer Students' Counter-Stories of Invisibility in Undergraduate STEM as a White, Cisheteropatriarchal Space. American Educational Research Journal, 59(5), 863-904. https://doi-org.libproxy.unl.edu/10.3102/00028312221096455

Type: Mixed Methods (Survey and Narrative inquiry)

This study specifically focused on Black queer undergraduate students in STEM and their “experiences of oppression and agency in navigating invisibility as STEM majors” (p. 863). There were 12 participants (four Black, four Latin, and four Asian) and all were queer, cisgender undergraduate students. There were several data sources such as a demographic survey, STEM autobiography, event journaling, and individual and group interviews. One of the largest findings was that “participants critiqued how ideologies of STEM as a neutral space organize professional norms that shape curricula where Black queer perspectives are erased” (p. 880). A quote from one of the students, Devin, succinctly shows why this is problematic, “society teaches us to consider people with all of these binaries and all of these boxes that we put people in. Those limit the kind[s] of products that we can design…People really like to act like engineering is so objective, and it’s not, because nothing is, but people acting like it’s more objective, they’re actually being worse. It’s reconfirming all of those biases because they don’t ever question them. (Interview 1)” (p. 883). The authors go on to state that “namely, ideologies of neutrality and objectivity in STEM shape institutional oppression of Black queer students through curricula that erase queer of color perspectives, including social justice issues in Black queer communities that are central to participants’ career motivations” (p. 888). This silencing and erasure of students’ identities not only harms those students’ pursuing careers in STEM, but it also harms the field’s ability to be creative and inventive to meet the needs of all people.

Linley, J. L., Renn, K. A., & Woodford, M. R. (2018). Examining the ecological systems of LGBTQ STEM Majors. Journal of Women and Minorities in Science and Engineering, 24(1), 1–16. https://doi.org/10.1615/jwomenminorscieneng.2017018836

Type: Qualitative (Interviews)

This study explores the impact of what the authors call “ecological systems” on queer students’ who are majoring in a STEM field. This was a qualitative study using student interviews coded by the researchers looking for themes. The participants were 60 students and were interviewed over the course of a weekend at a student conference. Of these, 15 were STEM majors and included in the study. The three themes identified from these interviews were: “(1) LGBTQ students experience multiple STEM microsystems influenced by faculty, peers, and colleagues; (2) social science and humanities academic microsystems are perceived as safe places for LGBTQ students to be out; and (3) students’ LGBTQ identities are central to their higher education experiences and influence peer microsystems in the context of exo- and macrosystem forces” (p. 1). The research is mixed on the experiences of LGBTQ youth in STEM fields, but there is consensus that “heterosexuality was routinely assumed” and “colleagues expressed discomfort about LGBT issues and people” (p. 5).

While all students spoke of the importance of acceptance within their chosen major by faculty and peers, far and away the most pivotal source of support for these students were the “LGBTQ peer microsystems on their campus…LGBTQ peers were identified as the most influential microsystem for most participants and those LGBTQ peers spanned disciplinary boundaries” (p. 10). This finding is important, because these students’ experiences of support within their chosen field was mixed and therefore not considered a consistent source of support and these students were turning to outside groups to support their identities within the STEM field.

Miller, R. A., & Downey, M. (2020). Examining the STEM Climate for Queer Students with Disabilities. Journal of Postsecondary Education and Disability, 33(2), 169-181. http://libproxy.unl.edu/login?url=https://www-proquest-com.libproxy.unl.edu/scholarly-journals/examining-stem-climate-queer-students-with/docview/2488225157/se-2

Type: Qualitative (Case study)

This study drew on data from a larger qualitative study focusing on the identities and experiences of 25 LGBTQ students with disabilities “at a large, predominantly white research university in the South. Of these five majored in STEM fields” (p. 171). These five students noted they experienced isolation and marginalization in STEM spaces in large part to lack of faculty support saying “faculty helped shape how students viewed themselves in the classroom and within their broader academic experience. Students struggled to build and maintain relationships with faculty because they said their identities would be ignored or ostracized by faculty” (p. 173). They noted that this was in large part to STEM’s male/masculine-oriented, heteronormative culture and their identities as queer, disabled students challenged this culture and its related norms. One student noted that “engineering climate is conservative and that she often chooses not to disclose her identities” (p. 175). Finally, these students pointed out that the campus was socially and physically inaccessible to them either because of construction or the bare minimum, or less, of accessibility requirements being met for things such as ramps and doors, leading students with mobility issues struggling to access classrooms, professor’s offices, or even basic supports such as the cafeteria or library. The students reported feeling “tokenized and isolated”, and the authors suggest that faculty should consider using universal design when designing curricula to ensure that all identities and perspectives are represented and that a feeling of belonging is created for all students (p. 178). The authors’ focus on belonging versus just inclusion is an important distinction as we work to welcome all students authentically. Not just including them because we were told to.

Miller, R. A., Vaccaro, A., Kimball, E. W., & Forester, R. (2021). "It's Dude Culture": Students with Minoritized Identities of Sexuality and/or Gender Navigating STEM Majors. Journal of Diversity in Higher Education, 14(3), 340-352. https://doi-org.libproxy.unl.edu/10.1037/dhe0000171

Type: Qualitative (Grounded theory)

This study on the inhospitableness of STEM towards students with minoritized identities of gender and/or sexuality used grounded theory to examine how 56 students “experienced and navigated a pervasive culture of centering cisgender heterosexual men within STEM environments—referred to by students as dude or bro culture” (p. 340). Of note, this was the only study from those I found that asked students to use their own words to self-report their gender identity and sexuality. Because of the variety of responses, the researchers used discrepant case analysis when looking at their data. Discrepant case analysis is described as “a deliberate and articulated search for disconfirmation and helps to combat the investigator’s natural tendency to seek confirmation” (p. 344).

Using this framework, the researchers found that students “identified a pervasive culture of centering cisgender heterosexual men within STEM learning environments…Some of the manifestations of dude or bro culture included: bonding over hypermasculinity and assumed heterosexuality, treatment of students with MIoSG as if they are inferior or invisible, and sexualizing and objectifying others” (p. 345). Students struggled to navigate this dude or bro culture based on their differing identities and found the learning environments not designed for them or welcoming. The authors go on to conclude that the first step to disrupting this pervasive culture is to recognize and name it, but merely making it visible is not enough to enact change. Meaningful change will only happen “when all campus leaders—across academic affairs, diversity, student affairs, and other portfolios—begin to identify, educate, and devote resources to deep-rooted change” (p. 349).

Suárez, M. I., Hawkman, A. M., Tofel-Grehl, C., MacDonald, B. L., Searle, K., Feldon, D. F., Sommers, T., & Hernandez, M. (2022). STEM as a Cover: Towards a Framework for Queer Emotions, Battle Fatigue, and STEM Identity. International Journal of Qualitative Studies in Education (QSE), 35(9), 960-979. https://doi-org.libproxy.unl.edu/10.1080/09518398.2022.2031333

Type: Qualitative: (Narrative inquiry and interview)

This study sought to understand the daily violence endured by queer youth in STEM fields based on their identity as queer. This was accomplished using Queer Battle Fatigue, Ahmed’s cultural politics of emotion, and STEM identity theories. The participants were 15 queer youth interviewed “over the course of a summer and fall LGBTQ+ maker camp in a rural town in the Intermountain Western part of the United States” (p. 960). The authors found that, “by providing queer campers an inclusive STEM environment, they more readily discussed their experiences at school vis-à-vis STEM identity’s competence, performance, and recognition, as campers’ STEM and queer identities developed positively” (p. 969).

The authors noted that the creation of this safe space for queer youth to explore their STEM identities in relation to their gender and/or sexual identities allowed for more expression of emotion, increased students receiving affirmation and advice, and created a sense of safety not often felt by these students at school or in the community. The authors concluded that “the findings from this study show that STEM teachers need not affix themselves or their classes to just teaching content. One can still teach about culturally responsive topics (e.g., pronouns, identity beads, masks we wear) while teaching STEM concepts without having to sacrifice one or the other” (p. 975). Throughout my research, it was noted time and time again that queer students sought out spaces outside of STEM to solidify and affirm their identities and this research is important because it is moving those safe spaces closer to the center. They’re making the center the margin.
