Fall 2023

TEAC 924J

Learning Reflection #1

Course reflection

Thus far, our focus in class has been on the intersections of poverty, race, gender, and sexuality in the STEM field, both in the classroom and in the field. Our first exposure to the severe inequities in the field was a report from the National Center for Science and Engineering Statistics that stated the field had gradually diversified in the last 10 years, the field was still dominated primarily by White and Asian men (2023, p. 3). The report did not address why these inequalities were still present and was quite reductive in nature, focusing only on gender or race or disability. The report chose not to focus on how those identities might intersect and shed light on some of the reasons the field is still relatively homogenous in terms of representation.

In the following weeks, we began to explore the root causes for the lack of diversity in the STEM field, and one of the most striking things to me was how early on students decided to pursue (or not) a major/career in STEM. While I found some elements of Gottlieb’s (2018) data collection problematic, she did agree with Dou et al. (2019) about “the importance of helping students visualize themselves as a person who participates and works in STEM or a related domain when seeking to increase their likelihood of pursuing careers in STEM” (p. 632). Dou et al. (2019) suggested more everyday experiences such as family conversations about STEM or exposure to books and TV shows when proposing ways to support a student’s STEM identity (p. 634) whereas Gottlieb (2018) focused more on the impact of “SES, math achievement, and expectancy-value constructs such as efficacy, attainment value, interest, and utility” as indicators of likelihood to pursue STEM (p. 1382). What both of these articles alluded to was the concept of identity and its impact on the formation of an additional identity surrounding STEM.

While the formation of STEM identity in typically marginalized groups is important work and crucial to helping diversify the field, we must also address the elephant in the room that former president of Harvard University, Lawrence Summers, stated clearly in a speech back in 2005, “In the special case of science and engineering, there are issues of intrinsic aptitude, and particularly of the variability of aptitude, and that those considerations are reinforced by what are, in fact, lesser factors involving socialization and continuing discrimination” (Beasley & Fischer, 2012, p. 427-428). Summers here asserts that there are in fact biological differences between genders and races that account for most, if not all, of the lack of diversity seen in STEM. Beasley and Fischer (2012), go on to argue however that “research has also provided evidence that the difference lies not in the aptitude of boys and girls, but in the different ways in which boys and girls believe intelligence and academic performance are related” (p. 429). Further supporting this, in Eliot’s book Pink Brain, Blue Brain (2009), the author explains that the ability to rotate shapes in space is one of the strongest mathematical predictors of success in STEM and that boys are usually stronger at this because of the culturally appropriate activities they participate in (p. 220). Eliot goes on to cite a Canadian study that exposed girls and women to video games designed to strengthen their spatial awareness and concluded that “this is experiment is key, because it shows that it’s the practice that actually develops the skill” (p. 233). Continuing this theme of video games as a potential equalizer, Gee (2007) discusses that video games create what is a called a psychosocial moratorium “that is, a learning space in which the learner can take risks where real-world consequences are lowered” (p. 59). He further challenges the reader to consider how a teacher might take this concept and apply it to their own classroom.

These first few weeks of reading set the stage into a more in-depth and critical look at how identity and intersectionality impact a student’s ability to form a STEM identity for themselves or sustain themselves within that identity in a field that forces them often hide or ignore large parts of themselves.

Berliner (2006) kicked this next section of our class off with a bang stating “poverty, race, and ethnicity are inextricably entwined in the United States” (p. 966). He goes on to say that “zip codes matter” and goes on to explain that “zip codes can determine school achievement as much or more than does the influence of a persons’ family, and they very often have much more power then [sic] the quality of the school a child attends” (p. 979). His argument is that the inequities we are seeing are institutional, cultural, and systemic and that the American myth of pull yourself by your bootstraps will not resolve the large issues and is the exception, not the rule. His concluding statement “attempting to fix inner city schools without fixing the city in which they are embedded is like trying to clean the air on one side of a screen door” is a reminder that we as teachers can do amazing equity work in our classrooms, but unless we address the larger systemic issues, we’re not doing much of anything (p. 988). Darling-Hammond in her chapter in Closing the Opportunity Gap (2013) charges that our primary focus should be a funding system that “must create a means for determining and funding adequacy, as well as incentives to increase the likelihood of funds being wisely spent” (p. 94). Both authors agree that these changes are unlikely to take place in our current system as it is not economically or politically advantageous to the dominant group (Whites) to lift up their fellow countrymen in poverty and ensure equitable access to education, among other things.

In subsequent articles such as Ong et al. (2018), the authors suggest the creation of counterspaces “closer to STEM’s center, because that is where more stakeholders and members with power can publicly address bias, exclusion and microaggressions” as a way of shifting the institutional culture of STEM (p. 236). Linley et al. (2018) have similar suggestions to address the discrimination and exclusion experienced by LGBTQ+ students (p. 12). These suggestions feel like band-aids on an open wound. Even in my presentation, I drew a distinction between micro and macro level changes and the importance of both, but I feel as though this is a bit of a cop out to assuage our guilty conscience as educators.

It is important, yes, to provide a safe classroom for our students and model for our peers what good teaching looks like, however, these micro-level changes are never going to be enough to address and repair the harm our educational system is causing those it professes to care for and serve. These everyday actions must continue because we are at least helping and protecting some students, but we need to put much more focus on an organized approach to restructuring the system as a whole. I realize that the following quote comes from a text outside of class, but I feel that it is important to acknowledge. From Boo’s Unapologetic (2018), she states “power concedes nothing without an organized demand” (p. 4) and I think this an important reminder as the field of education becomes more fragmented and deprofessionalized by the day. There is a large part of our political mechanism that wants to do away with public education and by fragmenting teachers’ unions, creating ridiculous measurement metrics, and striping teachers of their autonomy, they are trying to ensure that we as educators are not capable of forming an organized demand for change within the system. And even if we did, we have been so thoroughly painted as the villains that we are unlikely to find allies in parents and communities to stand with us.

This picture seems hopeless and the challenges insurmountable, but I turn back to the micro-level and the day-to-day classroom for hope. Educators are making a difference, even if only in their classroom. We need to celebrate these successes, look for commonalities both within and outside of the schools that enabled the successes, and build a grassroots community focused on meaningful and successful change. We just need to light the match. How do we support teachers who are already overworked and overwhelmed, and show the larger community the value in this work?
