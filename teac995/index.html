What this course was

Not a course. Research credit for a pilot study, taken because a dissertation should not be the first study you have ever run. So I asked someone I trusted. A friend and mentor of mine had come out of retirement to teach at an alternative high school in a smaller district inside a larger Midwest metro, and I asked whether I could come out two days a week, work in her classroom, and interview students about mathematics and technology — particularly through COVID. She said yes. The IRB said yes. The district’s superintendent said yes too, after helping me rewrite some of my more incendiary language into something more subversive, which got the approval and also taught me something I have used since: how to get what you want without compromising what you are actually saying.

Then I went. Twice a week for about a year, most weeks — it was a medically interesting year, and the missed weeks were not my idea. I helped teach. I helped grade. I tutored. Some days I was just the other adult in the room. Several students agreed to talk with me about their experiences with math and with the technology that had carried their schooling through the pandemic, and those conversations started well.

The problem was the school. Some students are there because they cannot manage the district’s modular high-school schedule — it runs like a college — and want a block schedule and a smaller room. Many more are there for attendance, or behavior, or credit deficiency. Absenteeism is not a variable in that building; it is the building. I would begin with a student and they would be gone for six weeks. Some did not come back at all. I ended the year with very limited data from the students themselves, and no product from the study except the one below — an article about research I had done earlier, in my own middle-school classroom, that I could only write the way I did because of the year in theirs.

Dissertation corpus

Through line

This is the study the dissertation refused to be. A pilot with real participants, real consent forms, a real IRB file — and a data set that thinned every week as the people in it stopped coming through the door. The dissertation that replaced it has one participant — me — and I am the only person in the design who can be relied on to show up. That is not the methodological argument for n = 1. It is the biographical one, and the comps questions ask for both.

It is also where the frame stopped being a reading list. TEAC 949B, a year earlier, had named the Freirean and de/colonial lens the work was already using. This building is where it crystallized — where you watch what the inequities do to particular students in a particular room and are left with no sensible way not to be radicalized by it. And where the country’s appetite for silver bullets, which I had called a myth on a discussion board in my first semester, was visible in a hallway as what it costs the students it is aimed at.

One more thing carried forward. The superintendent’s edit — incendiary into subversive, the claim intact — is the same move the dissertation makes about its own withholding: perform it rather than hide it. I learned it as a permission strategy. It turned out to be a method.

The research file for this study — IRB documentation, district approvals, consent materials, interview notes — is not published and will not be. It concerns students at a school that is not named here. What can be shown is the article.

Leveling Up for Equity

Sam E. B. Seim and Amanda Henson. Submitted to Teaching for Excellence and Equity in Mathematics (TEEM), the journal of TODOS: Mathematics for ALL. A practitioner article on a six-week gamification study in three Math 7 classrooms in spring 2022, written after the year at the alternative school. The abstract, as submitted:

This article describes the implementation of gamification strategies in three diverse Math 7 classrooms serving a high population of English learners and students of color. Through culturally responsive game design that honored students’ gaming experiences while embedding mathematical content, this approach increased student engagement and mathematical confidence without sacrificing academic rigor. The study demonstrates how gamification can serve as a tool for educational equity by leveraging students’ cultural assets and providing multiple pathways to mathematical success.
