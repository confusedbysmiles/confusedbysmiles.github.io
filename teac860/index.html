Production and Utilization of Instructional Materials

What this course was

An AI capstone course. When it kicked off I immediately thought about the Management Information Systems course I teach at Seton Hill. Having taught it before with materials I wasn’t thrilled about, I finally had the chance to really overhaul it instead of just getting handed last year’s export at the last minute. I rebuilt it for both my traditional undergrads and my adult learners, knowing full well I’d need to redo parts the next year because the textbook is so outdated.

I structured everything around a meta-cognitive framework to help students think like managers — not just analyzing data, but considering how systems integrate, their practical uses, and potential pitfalls. One thing that worked surprisingly well was sharing how I used AI to develop parts of the class. That created an accountability system for me and showed students real examples of AI in action, which is difficult to demonstrate in an asynchronous environment.

The course materials themselves — the Canvas template, the course data, and student exemplars — are not published here.

Through line

The final reflection for this course, written in April 2025, answers a prompt about which approach to AI proved most valuable: “as an assistant, because it could take my ideas and help me stretch and word them in ways that I would struggle to do on my own.” Eighteen months later, the plan I submitted to my committee before beginning comprehensive exams draws the same line in almost the same place — a transcriptionist and a note-taking assistant, not a generator of prose or ideas. I did not consult the reflection when I wrote the plan. I did not remember writing it.

The capstone does something stranger. Its pedagogical move was to show students how I had used AI to build their course, on the grounds that declaring the use is what makes it accountable. That is structurally the same act as the generative-technologies declaration comps require — except that in 2025 I invented it for my own classroom, and in 2026 an institution required it of me. The practice arrived before the policy did.

The reflection also names the moment it turned: vibe coding night. Not a reading, not a framework. An evening of building something that worked.
