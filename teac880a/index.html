What this course was

The survey course: one big idea a week for twelve weeks, a reading, a discussion board. Duckworth on the having of wonderful ideas. Pacey on technology as culture rather than kit. Mishra and Koehler on TPACK. Mitra’s hole in the wall and the Granny Cloud. Papert’s Mindstorms. Hunter’s World Peace Game. Perkins on teaching for understanding. Universal Design for Learning. A case study of edtech companies failing in India. The week-one folder holds the certifications I already had from before the program — Google Educator, Google Classroom, Apple Teacher — filed as evidence of a tech-savvy teacher who had just stopped being one.

I had left the classroom at the end of May. In my introduction post I said I was a data analyst now, tutoring part-time at a community college, and that I planned to return to education “in some form eventually.” I also said that what inspired me about teaching was “helping them see themselves as someone who can do math.” That is the first week of the first course. Three of the assignments are here: an autobiography of my own relationship with technology, delivered as a twenty-one-minute video; a survey of the technology in the district I had just left, delivered as an infographic; and a technology portfolio, of which the third entry — a walk-through of Classcraft, the gamified classroom-management platform — is the one I kept.

Dissertation corpus

Through line

The autobiography is the dissertation’s earliest primary source, and it does not know it. It was made in September 2022 as a course assignment: sit down, tell the story of how you came to technology, twenty minutes. The dissertation, as it now stands, asks a teacher to excavate the biographical roots of their practice — and here is the teacher, three and a half years before the dissertation was reframed to ask for exactly that, doing it on camera for a grade, in a course that wanted a technology story. What it got is what the dissertation now calls data. The person who made it was answering an assignment.

Before recording, I typed a list of 142 words to seed a word cloud. Technology appears twenty-three times, which is the assignment. Access appears six times — behind only technology, tool, and teaching, ahead of research, school, and learning. One of the dissertation’s six joint-display themes is Access. The theme was named in 2026. The list is from 2022.

The survey is the other half of the same year. It is the district I taught in for four years, described in the past tense five months after leaving it: staff laptops issued the autumn I arrived and still in use four years on, Chromebooks that went home in the summer of 2020 and often stayed there, a technology para with no required training, tools whitelisted at the district level that did not always work. The quotes are from an administrator, teachers, and students. The student one ends: “I stopped bringing my Chromebook to school because I never used it.” The literature review I was writing in the same semester has a section called Equity and Access that argues the same thing from the journals. This one argues it from the hallway.
