Annotated Bibliography: Gamification and Game-Based Learning in Mathematics

Annotated bibliography

Submitted to TEAC 995A in October 2022, six weeks before the literature review it fed. Eight sources. This is the clean copy, not the one returned with the instructor’s comments.

Engledowl, C., Al-younes, M., Chamberlin, B., & Stanford, T. (2021). Learning acquired from a computer game-based early algebra intervention. School Science and Mathematics, 121(8), 495–508. https://doi.org/10.1111/ssm.12500 This study focused on the impact of gamification of early algebra concepts on upper elementary students’ algebra knowledge and how that might impact their number knowledge that is typically taught in the US at these grade levels. The study itself was quite brief. Teachers attended two workshops on how to implement the game and were provided a structure for the three lessons they were two teach using this game. The authors did mention that this study was part of a much larger longitudinal study taking place and came out of some of the preliminary results of the larger study. This seemed to be much more of a “snapshot” of the potential for a longer study rather than something that results could be extrapolated and applied to other settings.

Possibly the biggest takeaway from this brief study is a closing remark from the authors “because the game environment allowed students to use mathematics in ways that matched their current understanding, they were able to develop their understanding naturally” which hints at the possibilities of effectively differentiated learning for students in a way that still engages them with the learning and provides opportunities for learners across a wide spectrum. Many of the authors discussed below explicitly or implicitly discuss technology, gamification, and equity and the implications of access on who gets to learn.

Lee, C.-Y., & Chen, M.-P. (2009). A computer game as a context for non-routine mathematical problem solving: The effects of type of question prompt and level of prior knowledge. Computers & Education, 52(3), 530–542. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2008.10.008 Lee and Chen focused on the impact that different types of question prompts, a student’s prior knowledge and the intersection of those things had on junior high students’

problem-solving performances on “non-routine mathematical tasks” or what we might think of as extension or application questions. They chose to implement the question prompts within the setting of a gamified classroom but other than mentioning that they used a pre-designed math game that they then altered, there were limited details on the game itself. This is a common theme in much research on gamification and is explicitly called out by Tokac, Novak, and Thompson (2019) in their article discussed below as something that needs to be improved in future research if the results are going to be verifiable and reproducible.

The study itself was a 6-week quasi-experimental study that worked with 78 9th graders from a public high school in northern Taiwan. The game itself was integrated into their normal lessons and in addition to their normal work. The results of the study were mixed.

While students who received specific, directed prompts did score better on the post-assessment than students who had received general prompts, the difference was not statistically significant. However, while question prompts and a student’s mathematical attitude did not predict performance on the simple tasks students encountered, those two variables did provide a strong indicator of performance on the more complex, non-routine tasks. Overall, they concluded that “therefore, successful use of computer games in the classroom depended on the quality of the teaching, including the teacher’s skill in diagnosing pupils’ abilities, identifying learning objectives and developing games in appropriate ways to meet these objectives” (p. 530). This again, was another common theme throughout my readings, that the technology or game had to be effectively integrated into the classroom environment or it could end up being detrimental to students and their academic attitudes and success.

Lei, H., Wang, C., Chiu, M. M., & Chen, S. (2022). Do educational games affect students' achievement emotions? evidence from a meta-analysis. Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 38(4), 946–959. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12664 Students’ emotions and attitudes around mathematics and their perceived ability are strong predictors of their academic performance in mathematics. Lee et al’s meta-analysis of 43 papers sought to tease out some common themes from the available research. They first explicitly defined an educational game as “innovative learning tools that typically have a main goal, sub-goals, student autonomy, immediate feedback, multiple tries, and a leader board” as they had noticed during their research that there was a lack of consistency within the current research on how terms were used and defined (p. 946). They spent a good deal of their time trying to standardize terminology to ensure that they were comparing and measuring effect sizes appropriately.

An interesting hypothesis put forward in their paper was that since “GBL [game-based learning] uses both extrinsic motivation (leader board, immediate feedback) and intrinsic motivation (autonomy, sub-goals), GBL might raise students’ PAEs [positive achievement emotion] and reduce their NAEs [negative achievement emotion] more in collectivist countries than in individual ones” and indeed they did find that this was the case (p. 948). This leads to an interesting set of questions about the efficacy of something like GBL within a highly individualistic country like the US. They did find, however, that regardless of how individualistic or collectivistic culture is students all reported enjoying GBL and had a statistically significant increase in PAEs and a similar decrease in NAEs. While not explicitly stated in this article, it was heavily inferred that for GBL to be successful, it needed to be authentically integrated into the existing curriculum and culture to have a positive outcome for students.

Offenholley, K. H. (2012). Gaming your mathematics course: the theory and practice of games for learning. Journal of Humanistic Mathematics, 2(2), 79–92. https://doi.org/10.5642/hummath.201202.07 This article was much more an introduction to gamification and examples of several different approaches. Offenholley starts by challenging the notion that work and play are diametrically opposed and goes on to state “Game Based Learning is an interactive pedagogy that has as its foundation the tenet that games, by their very nature, increase learning through positive emotional experience” and describes GBL as serious play (p. 79). A theme echoed by Wouters et al. and their discussion of “serious games.” One caveat that Offenholley does offer is that the choice of game is crucial to a student’s success. She discusses how games can offer the motivation to play and learn from within the game itself but that “the mathematics is subtle, and implicit, interwoven through the game, so it does not transfer well into a math class” (p. 89). She addresses this concern by tying back into a running theme throughout these articles that games must be a part of the classroom instruction and that careful guidance and questioning provides the scaffolding to keep a student in their ZPD and the desired state of flow.

Offenholley neatly sums up the appeal and potential of gamification by saying “for example, a game player can experience being a mathematician, not just by imagining that she is one, but by immersing herself in the language, structure, and actions of that field. Such immersion, says Shaffer, endows the work the player does with a deeper, epistemological meaning” (p. 87). We know that students require something to tie their learning to or they will not retain it and gamification itself can be that tie. So many students today play games in some capacity and Offenholley argues that we should leverage that to help facilitate student learning. One final note, Offenholley, like many others I read, cited the paucity of quality research in the field but with the potential seen in many of those studies that we would be remiss to not investigate gamification and its impacts further.

Shute, V., Rahimi, S., Smith, G., Ke, F., Almond, R., Dai, C. P., Kuba, R., Liu, Z., Yang, X., & Sun, C. (2020). Maximizing learning without sacrificing the fun: Stealth assessment, adaptivity and learning supports in educational games. Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 37(1), 127–141. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12473 This article was interesting because the authors seemed to lean into the implication that gamified learning is “stealth learning” and extended that concept to assessing and supporting student learning. The authors argue that keeping students in a state of flow is critical to the success of GBL and gamification and go on to say that this is done by developing and implementing “clear goals, achievable challenges and immediate feedback” (p. 129). In this study, the focus was on how stealth assessment and three different game conditions (adaptive, linear, and free choice) impacted students’ learning of basic physics concepts. This was another quasi-experimental design with 263 9th through 11th graders split into one control group and three different intervention groups. The experiment lasted just six days and students were introduced to a game called Physics Playground (PP) that was chosen for its ability to provide the teacher with student data without interrupting a student’s gameplay and used that data to provide feedback within the game. The three intervention groups were the three ways a student could interact with the game, the game adapted to their inputs, they played along a pre-defined linear path, or they could play in any order they chose.

As expected, the three intervention groups scored significantly higher on the post-assessment than the control group, however, the type of intervention did not seem to predict a student’s performance meaningfully. What was noticed was that the more students took advantage of the built-in physics animations completed more levels and earned more in-game rewards and this knowledge appeared to transfer to their performance on the post-assessment. These authors also explicitly examined the impact that race and gender might have had on a student’s perception of the game and post-assessment performance and noted that there were not statistically significant differences based on race or gender in student performance or enjoyment of the game. The author’s theorized that because they were able to maintain students’ enjoyment of the game by incorporating stealth assessments, they stayed longer in a state of flow and therefore learned more without sacrificing the data a teacher needs to inform their instruction and teaching practices.

Tokac, U., Novak, E., & Thompson, C. G. (2019). Effects of game-based learning on students' mathematics achievement: A meta-analysis. Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 35(3), 407–420. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12347

Tokac, Novak, and Thompson’s paper was another meta-analysis I chose because it neatly synthesizes a fairly scattered and inconsistent research field. Their specific focus was on the effect mathematical video games used in PreK - 12 curricula had on student achievement compared to more traditional non-video game-based instructional strategies. The authors noted, much like Lei et al., that the terminology used within the field of GBL and gamification is fluid and ill-defined, making it difficult to meaningfully compare studies and results. There was some disagreement even within this article on the effectiveness of GBL and gamification on student achievement. On the same page in the article, the authors state that “a small but marginally significant overall effect suggests that mathematics video games contribute to a higher degree of mathematics achievement compared with traditional instructional methods” and then just several sentences later go on to say “in other words, not all studies agreed on the effectiveness of video-gaming instruction compared with traditional instruction with respect to measures of mathematical achievement” (p. 415).

An interesting finding noted in their paper was that publication type (journal vs. thesis/dissertation) had a statistically significant variance in outcomes. According to the authors’ discussion “this [the data] implies that published studies tended to claim larger effectiveness of the video gaming intervention than their unpublished studies counterparts” (p. 417). This calls into question what agendas might be seen in published research that are perhaps not such an important factor in unpublished research such as a thesis or dissertation. This is somewhat offset as a concern when the authors go on to note that research is so scarce within the field that it is difficult to draw any conclusions. They mentioned that they started with a pool of 860 articles to pull from and after applying their inclusion criteria of publication year (2000 - 2017), implementation of both GBL and traditional instructional methods, and at least one classroom of each, the students were PreK - 12, that the games used could be qualified as good games by the criteria laid out by Shute & Ke (2010), and that there was enough data to calculate effect sizes, they were left with only 24 articles. This indicates strongly that more thorough research is needed in this area before any conclusions can be drawn.

Vandercruysse, S., ter Vrugte, J., de Jong, T., Wouters, P., van Oostendorp, H., Verschaffel, L., Moeyaert, M., & Elen, J. (2016). The effectiveness of a math game: The impact of integrating conceptual clarification as support. Computers in Human Behavior, 64, 21–33. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2016.06.004 In the Vandercruysse et al. study, their primary focus was on the impact of integrating conceptual clarification in a mathematical GBLE (game-based learning environment). This was a clustered, randomized study that took place with 108 VSE students in their 3rd and 4th years at school in Flanders, Belgium. It was a very short study, only lasting four hours, so much like Engledowl et al., this is much more a snapshot that a study where results and conclusions can be extended and generalized. Interestingly, compared to all the other articles discussed here, this was only one of two where the game was described in great detail. Both articles used the same game, Zeldenrust, a proportional reasoning game. The authors of this paper were actually the designers of the game because as they noted, there was a lack of high-quality, well-developed math games available for use, especially at low or no cost.

The interventions provided to the different groups of students were: reflection prompts, supportive information, and problem-solving practice beyond the game. The study found that all students regardless of intervention performed better on the direct application problems on the post-assessment after playing the game but that it did not indicate performance on the transfer or extension problems on the post-assessment. This is a similar finding to Offenholley’s observation that students can struggle to transfer their learning beyond the game if the mathematics incorporated into the game are too implicit and subtle. The final observation the authors made was that “GBLE is a learning tool for all students, nonetheless, students with basic arithmetic skills but without or with low proportional reasoning skills profit most from playing with the GBLE” (p. 29). Much like many of the articles discussed here, it was noted that GBL and gamification impacts students differently based on their prior mathematical ability and attitudes toward math in general.

Wouters, P., van Oostendorp, H., ter Vrugte, J., vanderCruysse, S., de Jong, T., & Elen, J. (2016). The effect of surprising events in a serious game on learning mathematics. British Journal of Educational Technology, 48(3), 860–877. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.12458

Finally, we come to Wouters et al. and their 2016 study on the impact of “surprising events” in a game on students’ learning. This study piggybacks directly off of the study completed by Vandercruysse et al. discussed above. Wouters et al. theorized that since “the articulation of knowledge and underlying rules is important, because it triggers students to organize new information and integrate it with their prior knowledge and thus construct a mental model that is more broadly applicable” if a “surprising event” was introduced, it might activate this mental model and enhance learning (p. 861). This study was structured similarly to its predecessor done by Vandercruysse et al. but split the students into two groups, one who experienced these surprising events and those who did not. These students were in their second year at a prevocational school in the Netherlands and 165 students participated in the study. The same pre- and post-assessments developed and used by Vandercruysse et al. were used in this study as well.

Much like the original study, Wouters et al. found that playing the game increased students’ performance on the first 12 questions of the post-assessment (those direct application questions) but that playing the game, with or without surprising events, did not increase performance on the last 4 questions (the transfer or extension questions). The authors theorized that because these students did not have well-developed cognitive flexibility and (meta)cognitive skills, they may not have had the skills necessary to fully adapt to the surprising events and therefore gained no benefit from them. They go on to suggest for future research, the development and implementation of specific instructional supports around developing cognitive flexibility and (meta)cognitive skills that would allow them to garner the benefits a surprise event might have on their gamified learning.
