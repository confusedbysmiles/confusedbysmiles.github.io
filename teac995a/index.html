What this course was

The first semester. Fourteen weeks of being told how a doctoral program works by people who had finished one: how to run a library search, what an IRB is for, which forms constitute a supervisory committee, how to read a research article without drowning in it. The first four weeks were Make It Stick — retrieval, spacing, interleaving, the illusion of knowing — which is to say the course opened by teaching us how learning works before asking us to do any. I completed the CITI human-subjects training in week five. I did not have human subjects. The dissertation would end up with exactly one, and it would be me.

The two graded pieces built on each other. An annotated bibliography in October — eight sources, each summarized and then argued with — and a literature review at the end of November that grew it to eighteen. The topic was gamification and game-based learning in the mathematics classroom, organized under three headings: what games do to achievement, what they do to attitude, and who gets access to them. The complaint that recurs across both pieces is that the studies are too short and too vague to trust — six days, three lessons, a game described only as “a pre-designed math game that they then altered.” Snapshots. Nothing that could be reproduced, and so nothing that could be built on.

Dissertation corpus

Through line

This was the dissertation. In the fall of 2022 I expected to spend the program studying the gamification of a mathematics classroom, and the literature review is the first draft of that study’s chapter two. The topic did not survive. What survived is on the first page, where I chose to open the whole argument with Offenholley’s line that “a game player can experience being a mathematician, not just by imagining that she is one, but by immersing herself in the language, structure, and actions of that field.” That is an identity claim. I filed it under engagement.

The third section is the tell. A review of game-based learning does not need a heading called Equity and Access, and mine has one, opened with Anderson, Weiland, Males, and Quaisley’s assertion that “all levels of teaching mathematics are imbued with the same racism and violence that permeates all schooling” — a week-twelve reading that went into the paper the same month. Whitton follows, on the “gaming literacy” a modern game presumes and the students it therefore excludes. Technology as access. Technology as gatekeeper. The first of my proposed comprehensive exam questions asks how technology figured in a teacher’s formation in exactly those two words, and I wrote them here, about other people’s students, four years before I wrote them about myself.

Two smaller things. The paper’s central objection — that a few weeks of intervention cannot tell you what a practice does to a person — is answered by a study that runs one participant for the length of a program. And the week-eleven readings included Olmanson, Kennett, and Cope on the techno-pedagogical pivot. He taught my second-semester course on AI in education. As of August 2026 he is my advisor.
