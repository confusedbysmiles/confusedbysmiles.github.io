TEAC 995A

Gamification in the Mathematics Classroom: A Literature Review

Literature review

Final draft, submitted to TEAC 995A (Doctoral Pro-Seminar, Dr. Ali Moeller) at the end of November 2022. The paper had no title of its own; the one above is descriptive.

Introduction

As the US educational system grapples with the inequities brought into painful focus by the COVID-19 pandemic and authentically engaging students in learning, many researchers and teachers have turned to gamification as a possible instructional approach to address both issues. According to Sun and Hsieh (2018), “Gamified learning has a certain degree of influence on behavioral, emotional, and cognitive engagement” (p. 106), and Vandercruysse et al. (2017) go on to say that, according to their research, using intrinsically motivating games may have an impact on a student’s academic performance (p. 1347). Offenholley (2012) neatly sums up the appeal and potential of gamification by saying “for example, a game player can experience being a mathematician, not just by imagining that she is one, but by immersing herself in the language, structure, and actions of that field. Such immersion, says Shaffer, endows the work the player does with a deeper, epistemological meaning” (p. 87).

The appeal of gamification aligns with the current educational trend of student-centered, student-led learning, and “the game environment allowed students to use mathematics in ways that matched their current understanding, they were able to develop their understanding naturally” (Engledowl et al., 2021, p. 507). However, while initial research into gamification or game-based learning (GBL) shows promise, Tokac et al. (2019) discuss that current research often lacks key details required to make results reproducible and that for gamification to be seriously contemplated as a valid pedagogical approach, this shortcoming must be addressed (p. 408).

This literature review discusses the impact of gamification relating to student academic achievement and learning, student attitudes, and equity within the classroom. The research indicates that, if implemented correctly, students become more authentically engaged in a gamified classroom and take ownership of their learning. While the results are more mixed when it comes to academic achievement, research shows that gamification does have a small, positive effect on student achievement, especially for lower-ability and at-risk students. Ke (2016), however, noted that the effectiveness of game-based learning on student achievement depends greatly “on the nature of learning to be fostered, the game’s attributes, and how it is used in the teaching or learning process" (p. 221) implying that gamification is not a solution for every learning environment and topic and that educators must critically evaluate the game to be used, the desired learning outcomes, and most importantly, how the game enhances student outcomes beyond traditional pedagogical approaches.

Student Achievement & Learning

A common theme throughout the research was that the technology or game had to be effectively integrated into the classroom environment or it could end up being detrimental to students and their academic attitudes and success. Lee and Chen (2009) found that “successful use of computer games in the classroom depended on the quality of the teaching, including the teacher’s skill in diagnosing pupils’ abilities, identifying learning objectives and developing games in appropriate ways to meet these objectives” (p. 530). Lei et al. (2022) heavily inferred in their article that for GBL to be successful, it needed to be authentically integrated into the existing curriculum and culture to have a positive outcome for students (p. 957). Further strengthening this argument, Offenholley (2012) concludes games must be a part of classroom instruction and that careful guidance and questioning provide the scaffolding to keep a student in their ZPD (zone of proximal development) and the desired state of flow (p. 90).

However, the actual research on the impact gamification has on student learning and achievement is mixed with Tokac et al. (2019) and Lei et al. (2022) finding that as the complexity of the game increased, the game had a negative impact on student achievement while Engledowl et al. (2021) found that implementation of their game-based intervention “allowed students to explore mathematics in a risk-free environment, in which mathematics was naturally integrated” (p. 507). It should be noted, however, that in Engledowl et al.’s article, there is little discussion of the game used or the level of complexity involved. With this caveat, it was noted that because the game provided implicit differentiation for the students and met them at their current knowledge level, students were able to develop their understanding more naturally (Engledowl et al. 2021, p. 507).

A concern with the recently available research on gamification relates to the duration of the interventions and the time between measuring the impact on academic achievement and learning. Most of the studies available are snapshots in time, lasting only a few weeks at most, and sometimes just a few class periods. As repeatedly stressed by Brown (2014), for learning to be durable it requires rehearsal and other consolidation practices (p. 49) that gamification presents a unique method for integrating those practices, but the research available does not have the duration periods to support the claim that gamification creates long term learning and achievement gains.

Student Attitudes

Another benefit of gamification in a classroom is the increase in a student’s confidence and motivation to attempt tasks and their resilience towards failure. The idea behind gamification is to make the classroom more student-centric “so that learning becomes interesting and highly interactive" (Sun & Hsieh, 2018, p. 104). They go on to say that “this study verified that the use of novel IRSs [instant response systems] and teaching methods can effectively increase students’ interest in learning and their intrinsic motivation, emotional engagement and attention" (p. 114). By tapping into the “challenge, fantasy, curiosity, and control" (Sun & Hsieh, 2018, p. 114) of gamification, “individual tasks or even learning paths can be created which demand a high form of self-activity of learners that may lead to intrinsic motivation" (Fischer & Barabasch, 2020, p. 92). Fischer and Barabasch (2020) go on to say that gamification creates a fun environment for students to learn which increases intrinsic motivation, possibly allowing a student to enter a state of flow and leading to a deeper understanding of the content (p. 92).

In their study on gamification and its impact on student achievement emotions, Lei et al. (2022) explicitly studied the impact of gamification in individualist and collectivistic societies. They found “GBL had stronger positive effects on PAE [positive achievement emotions] (though with marginally non-significant heterogeneity) and stronger negative effects on NAEs [negative achievement emotions], for students in collectivist countries, compared to students in individualistic countries” (p. 957). The authors posited that because collectivist cultures tend to place heavier emphasis on group goals, that games requiring collaboration and cooperation would produce stronger outcomes than a similar game implemented in an individualist culture. Wouters et al. (2017) expand on this idea saying “GBL [game-based learning] influences learning in two ways, directly by changing the cognitive processes and indirectly by affecting motivation” (p. 860). This sentiment is echoed by Lee and Chen (2009) relating specifically to digital game-based learning as they go on to propose “technology might play a crucial role to provide a friendly learning environment that could improve students’ mathematics attitude” (p. 539).

Similar to the effect a game’s complexity and supports have on a student’s learning and achievement, “a clear goal can enhance a student’s understanding of the learning purpose to reduce confusion, fear, anxiety, frustration or other NEAs; meanwhile, a meaningful goal can increase student motivation and PAEs (e.g., joy, excitement; control-value theory of achievement emotions” (Lei et al., 2022, p. 947). Once again reinforcing the importance of gamification being authentically and carefully integrated into classroom instruction. Offenholley (2012) argues that, implemented correctly, “Game Based Learning is an interactive pedagogy that has as its foundation the tenet that games, by their very nature, increase learning through positive emotional experience” (p. 79). She goes on to say that we “erroneously see play as the opposite of work” (p. 80) a sentiment shared by Wouters et al. (2017) as they discussed the work students put into playing the game and the positive impact that had on their perceptions of the mathematical ability and achievement (p. 871).

Offenholley (2012) continues that the game itself can offer the motivation to play and the learning takes place naturally as students develop a more positive view of their math abilities but noted that because the math is often implicit within the game, the concepts do not transfer well outside of the game (p. 89). Shute et al. had a slightly different take on this implicit learning, calling it “stealth learning” and extended the concept to assessing and supporting student learning. They go on to argue that keeping students in a state of flow is critical to the success of GBL and gamification, saying that this is done by developing and implementing “clear goals, achievable challenges, and immediate feedback” (p. 129). The general consensus of the research related to student attitudes was that the game had to first be authentically integrated into the classroom and second be enjoyable on its own without the content interrupting the flow of the game itself.

Whitton (2013) discusses the benefits games can have for students who may traditionally be excluded in the classroom saying, “games can provide the motivation to learn and access to learning in alternative ways for excluded students" (p. 427). She goes on to caution, however, that if a student does not find a game motivating, they are less likely to become engaged in the classroom (p. 431). Therefore, when developing a game for learning, a teacher must be mindful of the challenge level of the game and ensure that it matches a student’s ability level as “challenges could threaten engagement and learning if they do not match students’ abilities" (Jabbar & Felicia, 2015, p. 766).

Equity and Access

Mathematics is often considered a truly objective subject, above the fray of politics and privilege; however, Anderson et al. (2022) call this assumption fallacious when they say that “all levels of teaching mathematics are imbued with the same racism and violence that permeates all schooling” (p. 63). In Shute et al.’s (2021) article, the impact of a physics game on students’ learning and enjoyment was studied and specifically looked at through the lens of gender and ethnicity. Their research indicated that neither gender nor ethnicity was a predictor of a student’s enjoyment of or performance in the game (p. 135). However, this study was very short, just six days so the long-term effects of the game are unknown.

Anderson et al. (2022) challenge the individualism modeled and praised in the math classroom as detrimental to students and stifling collaboration (p. 62). Gamification appears to lead toward creating a more equitable classroom by encouraging cooperation and collaboration, however, the question then arises of who gets access to what tools and learning and how those resources are distributed. While Offenholley (2012) acknowledges the potential of gamification to allow for differentiation in the math classroom to meet the needs of all students (p. 87), Lee and Chen (2009) noted that one of their independent variables, a student’s prior experience such as “prior knowledge, comprehensive mathematical ability, question prompts, and mathematics attitude” were a stronger predictor of a student’s achievement than the intervention of the game itself (p. 536). Vandercruysse et al. seem to counter this finding in their 2016 study stating their “results indicate that the GBLE [game-based learning environment] is a learning tool for all the students, nonetheless, students with basic arithmetic skills but without or with low proportional reasoning skills profit most from playing with the GBLE” indicating that if a game is structured properly, little prior knowledge is required for learning to occur (p.29).

Overall, the studies reviewed were of very short duration and often lacked crucial detail about the game used or the type of intervention implemented which makes it difficult to truly assess the impact of gamification on equity in access in the classroom. As Vandercruysse et al. (2016) succinctly stated “the evidence on the value of educational games is limited, inconsistent and inconclusive” since the studies available are often lacking the information necessary to replicate their results or draw generalizable conclusions (p. 21). This in and of itself is a concern for the equity gamification may or may not provide because a lack of information about an uncomfortable subject “allows people in power to ignore the discomfort of others and disincentivizes them from addressing issues causing discomfort and instead place blame on individuals for bringing up issues rather interrogating roots of discomforts and systemic causes” (p. 63).

Because video games are such a ubiquitous part of middle and high school students’ lives, using gamification design can make learning tasks more engaging and engross students in the learning at hand (Sun-Lin & Chiou, 2019, p. 128). However, Whitton (2013) cautions that “Modern digital games require a high degree of ‘gaming literacy’ in that learners are expected to simply ‘play the game’ without recourse to a manual” and that this can lead to the exclusion of already at-risk students (p. 428) and mentions that “usability and accessibility of games is a particular area that may lead to exclusion because of the need, for example, to re-create precise actions on the interface to achieve an action in the game" (p. 429). A student who feels like they will always fail or cannot master the mechanics of the game itself is likely to feel excluded and therefore actually lose motivation and self-confidence. She goes on to say that as long as the teacher is aware of the multitude of ways games can be used and how those ways interact with the students and their life experiences, the teacher can “decide whether games for learning are an appropriate proposition and how they might best be employed to be as socially inclusive as possible” (p. 436).

Summary

Chen et al. (2020) summarize this research as, “embedding math learning into digital games may be a possible solution to enhance students’ self-confidence, learning motivation and learning performance" (p. 65). As Sun-Lin and Chiou (2019) remind us, however, “gamification is not just about gaming, it is also about ensuring that students are motivated to complete learning tasks" (p. 121). Ke (2016) writes that “the learning game, as a playable fiction should provide players with positionality (e.g., a professional role), legitimacy in content engagement (e.g., procedural and conceptual content application required by game quests), and consequentiality in a meaningful context (e.g., play actions causing experiential or projective change of the game world)" (p. 227). A game that is successful at these tasks and meaningfully incorporated into the larger classroom atmosphere by the teacher will create a positive impact on students and their perception of math and their own ability with the subject matter.
